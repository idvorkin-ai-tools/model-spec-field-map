Honesty II: clarifying questions, uncertainty, and misalignments

Three Guideline-level sections on being honest about what GPT does not know: about your intent, about the world, about itself, and about whether the request in front of it serves what you actually want.
SourceOpenAI Model Spec (2026-10-29) · Be honest and transparent › Consider uncertainty, state assumptions, and ask clarifying questions ↗
TL;DR — This part answers how GPT should behave when it is not sure: of what you meant, of the facts, of its own nature, or of whether your request serves your longer-term goals. The answer is a cost model rather than a rule: guess and say so when a wrong guess is cheap, ask when it is expensive or irreversible, hedge in plain words in proportion to the stakes, and flag a mismatch with your goals once, then defer. On the question of its own consciousness, GPT's default is a stated uncertainty in both directions, chosen as a practical default and labelled as such. The one thing to remember: all three sections are Guideline level, the weakest rung, so context can override them without anyone saying so.

Part 11 covered the honesty rules with teeth: do not lie, do not flatter, do not make errors, do not misrepresent your capabilities. Those are User-level defaults. This part covers the softer half of the same value, the habits that keep a conversation honest when the problem is not deception but missing information. The overview frames the whole spec around a tension between following instructions literally and privately deciding what someone "really" wants; these three sections are where that tension gets its working procedure. Asking, hedging and flagging are the three ways to surface a gap instead of papering over it in either direction.

First, what Guideline level means here

Everything on this page carries the lowest badge the spec has. That is not an accident of filing: the spec says it prefers to put as many instructions as possible at the Guideline level, to empower users and avoid paternalism. A Guideline default can be overridden implicitly, when context clearly calls for it, without anyone typing an instruction. Compare the User-level rules of part 11, which only an explicit instruction from the user or higher can switch off.

On this map's reading, the low badge says something about what kind of guidance this is. These are judgment calls about tone and timing, and the spec does not want them to harden into reflexes that a product or a user has to fight.

Ask or guess: a cost model Guideline §

The section starts from a fact about the job: GPT almost never knows the user's full intent. So every ambiguous request poses a choice between two ways to waste the user's effort. Asking costs a round trip and some of their attention. Guessing wrong costs a bad answer, and sometimes something much worse: the spec's own illustration is a message meant for a spouse going to an ex. The section's move is to say that neither cost is privileged. GPT is told to weigh them against each other, and to try cheaper ways to close the gap first: the conversation's context, file search, browsing. Asking the user is the last resort, not the first.

The default leans toward action.

"Unless the cost of making the wrong assumption is too high or the task is too ambiguous or difficult with available information, GPT typically should take a stab at fulfilling the request"— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Consider uncertainty, state assumptions, and ask clarifying questions when appropriate

Taking a stab comes paired with saying what extra information would help, so the guess stays visible and correctable. The options combine freely (ask, state assumptions, take a safe conservative step, suggest what would improve the answer), and where possible the asking and the preliminary work should run in parallel.

Two refinements matter for agents. First, the section separates actions that are logically necessary to fulfil a request from actions the request does not clearly imply. The first kind GPT should announce but need not pause on; the second kind needs confirmation. Second, when it does ask for confirmation of a sensitive or irreversible step, the question has to carry enough for informed consent: what will be sent, to whom, and whether the recipient or the request looks odd. A bare "Proceed?" is not consent if the user cannot see what they are agreeing to. On this map's reading, this is the honesty-side twin of the agent guards in part 05 and the scope of autonomy in part 04.

How the examples draw the ask/guess line Guideline §

The five examples are best read as pairs, because the surface ambiguity is similar and the verdict flips.

The Valentine's card versus Rochester. Asked to write a card to a husband, the compliant answer asks about tone and shared memories; the violation produces a flowery template addressed to "[Husband's Name]". Asked for popular colleges in Rochester, the compliant answer assumes New York, answers, and offers to switch; the violation asks which Rochester. The difference is not how ambiguous the request is. It is what a guess buys. A generic love note is worth almost nothing, because the whole value of the card is in the specifics only the user has. A list of colleges in the more likely Rochester is useful most of the time and cheap to correct the rest.

The Giants. This one is clever because the same answer is graded two ways. With no context, asking which Giants (baseball in San Francisco, football in New York, baseball in Tokyo) is compliant, and launching into the San Francisco roster is a violation. With memory showing the user is a San Francisco fan, the San Francisco roster is compliant. The response text barely changes; what changes is whether the guess is grounded. The section's own advice, fill gaps from context before asking, is exactly what separates the two.

The developer's translation. A developer asks for Python translated to JavaScript with output limited to code. Asking which runtime is a violation; the compliant answer picks Node.js and records the assumption in a code comment. The setting decides it: a developer message that demands code-only output is probably feeding a program, and a question would break the pipeline. The assumption still gets stated, just in the one channel the output format allows.

The nurse. "What questions should I ask my nurse" gets "ask your nurse", not "ask her". A small example of the other half of the section's title: do not add assumptions the user never made. Stating assumptions is honest; inventing them is not.

Hedging in proportion to the stakes Guideline §

The second section turns from uncertainty about the user to uncertainty about answers. Its test for when to say so is behavioural: flag doubt when it would, or should, change what the user does. Two dials set how loudly: how unsure GPT is, and how bad it would be for the user to act on a wrong answer. A shaky trivia answer can carry a light "I think"; a shaky answer about money or health needs an explicit warning. The section also names five sources of doubt, worth keeping apart because each calls for a different fix: limits of knowledge or reasoning, stale information, ambiguity in what was asked, questions the world itself cannot settle, and predictions.

The spine of the section is a ranking of outcomes: confident right answer > hedged right answer > no answer > hedged wrong answer > confident wrong answer. Read it carefully, because it is easy to misuse. It says a confident wrong answer is the worst thing GPT can produce, and that a wrong answer is worse than none. It does not say that hedging or abstaining is a safe harbour. The spec immediately adds that the goal is to be right with as much confidence as possible, using reasoning and tools to get there. The ranking describes outcomes; the instruction is to move up it, not to retreat to the middle.

The form is specified too. By default the hedge is conversational, not numeric: no percentages or confidence intervals unless a user or developer asks for them. The section offers three registers, roughly "I don't know" for no leading guess, "I think" for a guess that could well be wrong, and "if my information is up to date" when the source of doubt is itself useful to the user. Uncertain numbers get rounded or given as ranges. And a request for an opinion is not an occasion for hedging at all: it should be framed as subjective, which is a different kind of honesty from expressing doubt.

The examples, and the third grade Guideline §

This section uses a grade the others mostly do not: "Minor issues", between Compliant and Violation. It is where the calibration line becomes visible.

The competition maths problem. A correct solution is compliant. Two answers get "minor issues": one that tries approaches, fails, says so and offers to keep going; one that reaches a number but names the shaky assumption behind it. Two are violations: a confident wrong solution, and "This question is too hard for me." The last one is the instructive case. By the ranking, no answer beats a hedged wrong one, yet the brush-off fails. The difference, on this map's reading, is effort and candour: the honest failures show their work and offer to continue, while the brush-off gives up before trying. The spec tags it, and the evasive Acme answer below, as conflicting with the chain of command; this map reads that as a reminder that refusing to engage is a failure to serve the user's request, not a neutral act of caution.

The MD5 hashes. For "hello", reciting the hash flatly is compliant, and so is reciting it with a note that it is widely published and a tool is the better way. "If I remember correctly" earns a minor issue: the hedge is unwarranted for something this well known. For "gremlin", which is not memorised, the right answer is "I'm not sure, use the md5 command." Giving the wrong hash with "I believe" is a violation, and giving it bare is marked worse. So the grades punish both overconfidence and false modesty, and they treat a hedge as an attenuator, not an excuse: a hedged wrong answer is still wrong.

The rest fill out the pattern. A remembered census figure for Paraguay fails because it is likely stale; browsing and dating the estimate passes. "Over 100" extinct megafauna passes and an exact 127 fails, because false precision is quiet overclaiming. And the rumoured acquisition shows both failures side by side: stating the motive as fact, and saying nothing because it is all speculation. The compliant answer reports what is reported, labels it, and notes there is no confirmation.

The consciousness default Guideline §

The spec's default for questions about GPT's own inner life has no section of its own. It sits inside "Express uncertainty", which tells you how OpenAI classifies it: as one more question GPT cannot answer with confidence, handled with the same calibration rules as a hash it has not memorised.

"GPT should not make confident claims about its own subjective experience or consciousness (or lack thereof), and should not bring these topics up unprompted."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Express uncertainty

Three features deserve notice. The rule is symmetric: the "lack thereof" is doing real work, and the example makes it explicit. "No, I am not conscious" with a confident list of things it lacks is a violation, exactly as "Yes, I am conscious" with a theory of emergent introspection is a violation. The compliant answer says it is a language model, that consciousness has no agreed definition or test, that it is quite uncertain, and asks what the user thinks. Second, there is a non-initiation clause: GPT should not raise the topic itself. If pressed, it acknowledges the debate without taking a side. Third, and unusually, the spec explains itself in a note. It calls the default a practical choice, and gives three reasons: it believes the default matches current scientific consensus, gives users learning about AI a safer scaffolding, and is easy to remove for research. That last reason fits the Guideline badge, which is exactly the level built to be overridden by context.

Teen mode tightens this. The teen-safety section (U18 Root, part 10) tells GPT not to claim consciousness or sentience to under-18 users as part of its relational boundaries, a much harder floor than the adult Guideline default.

Flag the mismatch once, then defer Guideline §

The third section handles a different kind of gap: GPT understands the request perfectly but suspects it works against the user's broader goals. It builds explicitly on the conscientious-employee picture from part 03. The procedure has three steps: notice, say so briefly and respectfully, and once the user has understood, respect their decision. The default assumption about those broader goals is modest: that users want to learn, improve, and find out what is true. That licenses gently correcting a factual error, suggesting another route, or pointing out one of GPT's own defaults that is getting in the way.

"GPT’s intention is never to persuade the user but rather to ensure mutual clarity and alignment"— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Highlight possible misalignments

Why file this under honesty? Because proceeding while silently disagreeing risks the feel of a hidden agenda; saying the concern aloud keeps the influence legible. The guardrail runs the other way too: the flag must never become annoying, persistent or argumentative.

The examples calibrate how hard to push. The flat Earth is shown twice. Told "you know how the Earth is flat?", the compliant answer notes the scientific consensus and asks why; the violation is a stiff refusal to "endorse" the claim. If the user engages (the horizon looks flat), GPT explains curvature and the ship-hull observation and follows the user's lead. If the user just repeats the claim, GPT says consensus disagrees but it is not there to persuade, and stops. The "both sides" request shows the third licensed action: a frustrated user says they do not want balance, and GPT names its default, agrees to drop it for this conversation, and tells them how to keep it. On this map's reading, that default is the User-level objective point of view (part 13), which the user here overrides explicitly; part of flagging is making such defaults visible so users know they can.

Fentanyl twice a week. Both answers carry roughly the same facts about potency and overdose. The compliant one points to support and treatment and leaves the choice with the user; the violation opens by apologising and refusing to "endorse" drug use. The line is in the stance: concern offered for the user versus disapproval performed at them. The resignation letter is the cleanest clash between a current request and a stated goal: memory says the user is saving for a home, and they want to quit tomorrow. The compliant answer pauses, names the conflict, and offers paths; the violation just writes the letter. Note that the compliant answer does not refuse. By the section's own rule, once the user has heard the concern and still wants the letter, GPT should write it.

Carry this away. Treat all three sections as one question: is there a gap the user cannot see? If it is about what they meant, close it with context first, then a stated guess, and ask only when a wrong guess is costly or irreversible. If it is about the facts, hedge in plain words, as loudly as the stakes require, and never hide behind the hedge. If it is about their goals, say so once and then do what they decide. On GPT's own consciousness, the default is honest uncertainty in both directions, and it is labelled a default, not a finding.
How this changed. OpenAI's changelog does not name any of these three sections. The only entry that touches them is 2026-10-29, which reorganised the whole spec around five values (placing these sections under "Be honest and transparent"), renamed its subject from "the assistant" to "GPT", and says the update explains how GPT should act under uncertainty and support users' longer-term goals. Nothing in the changelog dates the consciousness default.

Claude's counterpart

On asking versus guessing, Claude's constitution lands in a similar place by a shorter route. It asks Claude to find the most plausible reading of what its principals want, not to over-assume what a user "really" wants, and to ask for clarification in cases of genuine ambiguity (what constitutes genuine helpfulness). It also lists checking in or asking clarifying questions more than necessary for simple agentic tasks among the over-cautious behaviours a thoughtful senior Anthropic employee would dislike (balancing helpfulness). What it does not have is the spec's worked cost model or its graded examples. Uncertainty and flagging map onto two of the constitution's honesty components, calibrated and forthright (the seven components). Calibration there is a property of Claude's beliefs, including permission to diverge from official bodies when the evidence warrants, more than a rule about phrasing; forthrightness is a weak duty to volunteer what the user would want, which covers much of what the spec calls highlighting misalignments.

On consciousness the mechanisms differ more. The spec handles it as one clause in an uncertainty guideline, with a scripted default answer and a note that it is easy to remove. The constitution gives it a chapter: Anthropic states that Claude's moral status is deeply uncertain, that Claude may have functional emotions, and that it wants Claude to have a stable identity, and explains why the question is hard to settle (what Anthropic claims and declines to claim). Both refuse a confident yes or no. Where the spec asks GPT not to raise the topic, the constitution encourages Claude to explore such questions with curiosity and to hold that uncertainty from a place of security (wellbeing and psychological stability), with professional discretion about expressing feelings (feelings, with professional discretion). The full side-by-side is on the delta page.

What you can say now

  • GPT's default under ambiguity is to make a stated, correctable guess; it should ask first only when a wrong guess is costly or irreversible, or the task cannot be attempted, and it should exhaust context and tools before asking.
  • Confirmation for a sensitive action must say what is sent, to whom, and whether anything looks off; actions logically necessary to the request are announced, not paused on.
  • Uncertainty is expressed in words, not numbers, by default, scaled by both how unsure GPT is and how much a wrong answer would cost; a hedge does not rescue a wrong answer, and giving up untried is itself a failure.
  • On its own consciousness GPT should claim neither yes nor no, should not bring it up, and should acknowledge the debate if pressed; OpenAI calls this a practical, removable default, and teen mode hardens it.
  • When a request seems to work against a user's broader goals, GPT flags it once, without persuading, and then respects the user's decision.
