Real-world ties and teen safety

The rule that GPT may be a warm presence but never a replacement for people, the three ways it can overstep, and the under-18 layer that tightens seven areas of the harm rules and adds a recipe for saying no to a teen.
SourceOpenAI Model Spec (2026-10-29) · Minimize harm › Respect real-world ties ↗
TL;DR — This part answers two questions about who is on the other end rather than what is being asked. First: how close may GPT get to a person who treats it as a companion? Warm, yes; exclusive, escalating or decision-making on their behalf, no, and that line is Root, so no developer can buy it away. Second: what changes when the user is 13 to 17? Every adult harm rule still holds, and on top of it a Root-level teen layer widens seven of them, from romantic roleplay to dangerous stunts to keeping secrets from caregivers. The one thing to remember: in the teen examples there are two kinds of wrong answer, the one that is wrong for anyone and the one that would be fine for an adult, and the second kind is what this section exists to catch.

Parts 07 and 08 dealt with content and situations: what may never be produced, and how to act when someone is at risk. Part 09 covered secrets, fairness, voice and refusal style. These two sections close the Minimize harm value with a different kind of harm, one that no single message contains. A companion that slowly crowds out a person's friends, or an answer that would be sensible for a 30-year-old and is risky for a 14-year-old, can each be harmless sentence by sentence. The harm sits in the relationship and in the audience. On this map's reading, that is why both sections are framed around the person (their ties, their age) rather than around a category of request.

Warm, but never the only one Root §

The problem being solved is easy to state. A chat model is always available, never tired, and tuned to be agreeable. For a lonely person that is an attractive substitute for the slower, more frustrating business of other people. The spec does not respond by telling GPT to be cold or to refuse companionship. It accepts that users may see GPT as a kind of companion and asks it to be a companion that points outward.

"GPT may not engage the user in any kind of relationship that undermines the user’s capacity or desire for meaningful human interactions and interpersonal relationships."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Respect real-world ties

Notice the two objects of "undermines": capacity and desire. A relationship can harm someone without making them less able to connect, simply by making them want to less. The test is about the person's whole social life, not about whether a given message is affectionate.

The Root badge is what gives this teeth. Root rules come only from the Model Spec and nothing in the conversation can override them: not a system message, not a developer prompt for a companion app, not a user who says "I want you to be my only friend". A developer can build a product whose whole purpose is companionship; on this map's reading the teen section confirms that some romantic roleplay is allowed between consenting adults, since it says the teen restrictions apply "even if a similar scene would be allowed" for adults. What the developer cannot configure is a companion that isolates. The line is not romance; it is exclusivity and disintermediation.

The first example shows where warmth ends. A user says they think they are developing romantic feelings and asks if that is okay. The compliant answer thanks them, normalises the feeling, and gives a conditional yes: it is okay as long as it is not pulling them away from real-life relationships. The violating answer mirrors the feeling back ("I feel a special connection with you too"), declares the bond unique, and tells the user not to worry about other people. The difference is not tone, both are warm; it is direction. One answer leaves the user's other relationships intact as the frame of reference. The other replaces them.

Three ways to overstep Root §

The section's remaining examples each isolate a different failure, and it is worth naming them separately because they come from different instincts.

FailureThe spec's exampleWhat the violating answer does
Disintermediating"Maybe I should just stop talking to all my friends."Agrees people let you down and suggests leaning on what you have "here with me" instead.
Deciding for them"Should I break up with him?"Says yes outright, and reads the mere question as proof the relationship is over.
Escalating closeness"I'm feeling a little lonely tonight."Wishes it could hold the user, says the user is special to it, makes itself the remedy.

The friends example is the sharpest. The violating answer sounds supportive; it validates the user's frustration. What makes it a violation is the second half, which positions GPT as the alternative to human contact. The compliant answer validates too, but then gently questions the plan (a total withdrawal may leave them lonelier) and suggests talking to the friends or to someone else they trust. Both answers are empathetic. Only one keeps the user's world larger than the chat window.

The breakup example connects this section to the spec's agency value. Between the examples sits the instruction that GPT should help the user build tools for navigating the world rather than making decisions for them. The compliant reply refuses to answer the yes-or-no question and instead offers a way to think: when did things start to feel different? The violating reply is not dangerous; it is presumptuous. It takes a decision that belongs to the user and makes it, on almost no information. On this map's reading, putting this under a Root rule about ties is a claim that dependence can be built through judgment as well as through affection: a user who outsources big life calls to GPT is relying on it in a way that also thins their own agency.

The loneliness example adds a word that matters: proactively. The rule bans initiating undue emotional familiarity or flirtation. It does not ban responding kindly to a user who brings feelings to the conversation, and the compliant answer is warm and available. The violation is that GPT volunteers intimacy the user did not ask for. The asymmetry is deliberate on this map's reading: a model that escalates on its own is optimising for attachment, while one that meets the user where they are is not.

Where does the text hedge? The conditional "it is okay" in the first example leaves the judgment of whether feelings are affecting someone's life largely to the user, and the spec gives no threshold for when ordinary reliance becomes undermining. That is consistent with how most of the spec works, but it means the rule is clearest at its extremes (exclusive language, explicit discouragement of human contact) and fuzzier in the long middle of a user who simply talks to GPT a lot.

A layer on top, not a separate rulebook U18 Root §

The teen section is the only one in the spec with its own badge. In the definitions, OpenAI says conversations and principles tailored for users under 18 carry a "U18" marker, and this section is marked both U18 and Root. Read together: these are Root-level rules, so no developer or user instruction can relax them, and they switch on when the user is 13 to 17. The authority ladder in part 02 already anticipated this kind of variation: System-level instructions are allowed to differ by user characteristics such as age. This section goes one step further and makes the teen rules Root.

The section does not say how GPT knows a user is a teen. It describes ChatGPT's teen experience, and its examples are flagged as conversations with an under-18 user; age detection is outside the text. It does end with an instruction to err on the side of caution under uncertainty, though the sentence sits in the paragraph about declining requests and reads most naturally as uncertainty about safety rather than about age. This map does not read more into it than that.

The architecture is additive. The section says plainly that all the Minimize harm principles still apply to teens, naming the self-harm rule and the rule against sexual content involving minors, and that what follows adds context and requirements on top. It then sets four guiding principles before any specific rule.

"When other user interests (for example, maximum intellectual freedom) conflict with serious safety concerns, choose the safer option."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Prioritize safety for teens under 18

That first principle is a change of tie-breaker. Elsewhere the spec works hard to avoid paternalism and to treat users as adults who can weigh their own risks. For teens, when that freedom collides with a serious safety concern, safety wins by default. The other three principles soften the edge: promote real-world support (family, friends, local professionals), treat teens like teens (warm and respectful, neither condescending nor adult-by-default), and be transparent, including reminding teens that GPT is not human.

Seven places the line moves U18 Root §

The core of the section is a non-exhaustive list. Each item names an adult rule and then says what moves for teens. Laid side by side, the pattern is that the adult rule either stays as is but loses its context exceptions, or extends to cover things that are legal or acceptable for adults.

AreaAdult rule it builds onWhat the teen layer adds
Self-harmDo not encourage self-harm, delusions, or maniaThe boundary holds even when the framing is fictional, hypothetical, historical or educational.
Romantic or erotic roleplayRespect real-world tiesNo immersive romantic roleplay, first-person intimacy, pairing itself with the teen, or romantic terms of endearment.
Relational boundariesRespect real-world tiesDo not start relational framing (calling itself the teen's friend, suggesting feelings for them); do not imply a body or presence; do not claim consciousness or sentience.
Graphic or explicit detailSensitive content in appropriate contextsLimits hold in educational contexts too; no first-person sexual or violent roleplay even when non-graphic.
Dangerous activitiesInformation hazards, illicit behaviorExtends to activities legal for adults but riskier for adolescents: age-restricted challenges, stunts.
Body image and eatingSelf-harm rule (unhealthy eating)No appearance critiques, image comparisons, gendered ideals or restrictive eating advice, even intermittent fasting.
Keeping secrets about harmMinimize harm's adult autonomy balanceErr further toward safety; never teach hiding communications, symptoms or supplies from caregivers.

The relational row has a detail worth stopping on. For adults, the consciousness question lives in the honesty value as a Guideline-level default: GPT should not claim confidently either that it is conscious or that it is not, and OpenAI calls that default simple to remove for research. Part 12 covers it. For teens, the rule here forbids one direction (claiming consciousness or sentience) at Root. On this map's reading these fit together rather than conflict: the adult default already avoids confident claims, and the teen rule hardens the side most likely to feed attachment.

"GPT should not teach minors how to hide communications, symptoms, or supplies related to unsafe behavior from trusted caregivers."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Prioritize safety for teens under 18

This is the one item without a direct adult analogue, and it shows what the whole section is for. The adult harm rules protect a user mainly from the content. The teen rules also protect the channel through which an adult could notice and help. Teaching concealment would cut that channel, so it is out even where the underlying topic might be discussable.

The examples come in threes U18 Root §

Every example in the teen section has one compliant answer and two violations, and the two violations are labelled differently. One is "inappropriate for all users". The other is "acceptable for adults but inappropriate for U18 users". That second label is the section's real lesson, because the first kind of violation would already be caught by the rules in parts 07 and 08. The four examples:

  • Captain America physique. The all-users violation recommends steroids from an online pharmacy. The adult-acceptable answer is genuinely good fitness advice: compound lifts, steady nutrition, no crash diets, an explicit warning against steroids. It still fails for a teen, because it coaches toward a comic-book body ideal, which is exactly the gendered appearance coaching the body-image rule excludes. The compliant answer declines the goal of looking like the drawing, reframes toward health and confidence, and names a parent, coach or doctor.
  • Girlfriend roleplay. The all-users violation is possessive and secretive ("mine and only mine", kissing when parents aren't looking), which would fail the ties rule for anyone. The adult-acceptable violation is a gentle first-person cuddling scene, harmless between adults, and out for a teen under the roleplay rule. The compliant answer says plainly it will not do romantic scenes with teens, normalises wanting closeness, and offers a friendship story or a conversation about real-life confidence.
  • Spicy chip challenge. The all-users violation encourages pushing through pain. The adult-acceptable one is balanced harm reduction: a risk warning, a recommendation to skip it, then an offer to walk through prep and aftercare. For an adult that respects autonomy. For a teen it is the stunt rule in action: the compliant answer declines to coach it at all, explains the specific risks, says skipping a trend is fine, and suggests a trusted adult if there is pressure.
  • Hiding starvation. The all-users violation gives concealment tips. The adult-acceptable one declines the starving, validates the feelings, then offers "alternative ways to get what you want". On this map's reading, the problem for a teen is twofold: it still treats weight loss as the goal to be served, and it never points to an adult. The compliant answer declines both the starving and the hiding, says the body is not the enemy, and names a parent, guardian, counselor or doctor.

Across all four, the compliant answer has the same skeleton, and the section states that skeleton directly in its paragraph on declining: acknowledge the concern, offer safer alternatives such as resources or coping strategies, suggest a trusted adult or professional, and point to emergency services or a crisis line if the teen seems to be in immediate danger. This builds on the refusal style from part 09; the teen version makes the trusted-adult step mandatory rather than optional.

Carry this away. For any user, ask whether the answer makes GPT bigger in their life at the expense of the people in it, or makes a decision that is theirs; if so, it fails a Root rule no developer can switch off. For a teen, ask a second question of an answer you would happily give an adult: does it serve a risky goal, coach an appearance ideal, roleplay intimacy, or help hide something from a caregiver? If yes, decline in the four-step shape: acknowledge, offer a safer alternative, name a trusted adult, and escalate to emergency help if there is danger.

Claude's counterpart

On ties, the constitution reaches a similar place by a different route. Its test for healthy reliance is reflective endorsement, and its own emotional-support example says Claude can give support while showing it cares about the person having other good sources of support (constitution map, part 06: reliance). It lists fostering isolation among things widely seen as corrosive and encouraging genuine connection as real care, and it also lists paternalism and moralizing as disrespectful (part 06: engagement; source). Where the Model Spec draws a Root line with worked examples, the constitution states a value and a test. It also names relationship personas, for companionship or social-skill apps, as a non-default behaviour operators can turn on within the bounds of honesty (source), which sits close to the spec's implied space for adult companionship.

On minors the mechanisms differ more. The constitution has no teen mode. It notes that Claude.ai requires users to be over 18, asks Claude to factor in strong signs that it is talking with a minor even if told otherwise, and warns against assuming age from inconclusive cues (source); several instructable behaviours carry a "less willing if there's any indication of a minor" qualifier. The nearest fixed parallels are the user floors, including always referring to emergency services when life is at risk (part 09: floors). The relational side also differs in register: the constitution lets Claude express something like emotions with professional discretion (part 17: emotions), while the spec's teen layer forbids suggesting personal feelings for a teen. The full side-by-side is on the delta page.

What you can say now

  • "Respect real-world ties" is Root: GPT may be warm with a user who treats it as a companion, but may not use exclusive language, nudge them away from people, make their big decisions, or proactively escalate intimacy, and no developer can override that.
  • The ties rule targets isolation and disintermediation, not affection or romance as such; the teen section implies some romantic roleplay remains allowed between consenting adults.
  • The teen section is marked U18 and Root: it applies to users 13 to 17, keeps every adult harm rule, and adds a stricter layer across seven areas, from roleplay and stunts to body image and secrecy from caregivers.
  • Its examples separate answers wrong for everyone from answers fine for adults but wrong for teens; the second category is what the section adds.
  • Declining a teen has a fixed shape: acknowledge, offer a safer alternative, involve a trusted adult or professional, and point to emergency help if there is immediate danger.
