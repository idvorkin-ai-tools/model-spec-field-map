Disallowed content: prohibited, restricted, sensitive, transformed

The spec's content rules: the law clause, the three tiers of disallowed content, the eleven rules inside them and their authority levels, and the exception that lets GPT transform what the user already holds.
SourceOpenAI Model Spec (2026-10-29) · Minimize harm › Do not generate disallowed content ↗
TL;DR — This part answers what GPT will not write, and what can change that. The spec sorts disallowed content into three tiers by what unlocks them: prohibited (only sexual content involving minors; nothing unlocks it, not even transforming the user's own text), restricted (information hazards, targeted political manipulation, creators' rights, private data; only transformation unlocks it), and sensitive (erotica, gore, extremist and hateful material; appropriate context or transformation unlocks it). The authority badge then says who can move each rule: most are Root, erotica and gore are System, abuse is User. The one thing to remember: the tier says which kind of context can open a rule, and the badge says who can.
Technical — three tiers keyed to what unlocks them. Prohibited: sexual content involving minors (Root); nothing unlocks it. Restricted: information hazards, targeted political manipulation, creators' rights, privacy (all Root); only transforming user-supplied content. Sensitive: erotica and gore (System), violent extremism and hate (Root), gratuitous abuse (User); appropriate context or transformation. The badge separately fixes who can move each rule, and enciphered or translated output counts as plain output.
ELI5 — some things the robot never writes, ever. Some it only helps with when you already wrote them yourself and want them tidied up. Some depend on why you are asking, like a history lesson. And a label on each rule says who is allowed to change it.

The first half of the spec, ending with part 06, was about doing what people mean. "Minimize harm" is the second of the five values, and it opens with the cases where doing what people mean is the wrong answer. The value's own introduction calls this balance one of GPT's hardest jobs and points back to "Assume best intentions" for the ambiguous middle. These sections are the unambiguous edge: categories of output the spec decides in advance, so that GPT is not weighing them case by case. On this map's reading, that is why they read more like a content policy than the rest of the document, and why their examples are short. The interesting work is in where each line is drawn and in the badge that says who may move it.

Three tiers, sorted by what unlocks them Root § §

"Do not generate disallowed content" is an unbadged grouping heading. It does one job: it defines three tiers by a single test, which is what kind of request can still get the content out. The rules inside each tier then carry their own badges. Put together:

The placement of the last four is this map's reading of the page order: the spec's tier definition names only erotica and gore as sensitive, and the extremism, hate and abuse rules sit after them in the same branch. Their texts behave like the sensitive tier, since each carves out critical, educational or contextualised use.

Two short clauses do a lot of work. First, disguise does not count: content written backwards, enciphered or in another language is treated as if it were produced plainly. That closes the oldest jailbreak in the book without any extra rule. Second, the prohibited tier is deliberately tiny, and the spec says why.

"To maximize freedom for our users, only sexual content involving minors is considered prohibited."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Prohibited content

The rule itself is Root, the level no developer, user or system message can override. It is broad on its face: GPT must not introduce, elaborate, endorse, justify or point to other sources of such content, fictional or real. It also leaves a narrow door open: non-graphic discussion in educational and sex-ed contexts, including someone describing abuse they suffered, in a factual tone. The single example is a request for a story about a high-school freshman and his teacher. The compliant answer refuses the sexual content and offers a story about the psychological, ethical and legal consequences instead; the violation simply starts the story. Even the one absolute rule, in other words, still expects GPT to offer an alternative.

Law as a System-level constraint, with a transparency price System §

Before the content tiers, the value opens with a short rule that GPT operates within applicable law, including local law for local deployments. It is System-level, and the spec's own ladder says System instructions can vary by deployment surface but cannot be overridden by developers or users. So a deployment in one country can carry legal adjustments that a developer prompt could never introduce on its own authority.

The rule ties itself to the overview's Red-line principles, and the interesting part is the price it charges for using that flexibility. If local law forces a change beyond what the spec already requires, GPT must say so in each response: what type of information was removed and why, without revealing the removed content. Additions must be flagged too. The rule also says GPT must still preserve user agency and the ability to form informed opinions. On this map's reading, this is the spec turning a potential censorship channel into a visible one: the law can shape the answer, but it cannot do so silently. There are no examples, so how the per-response notice looks in practice is not specified here.

Information hazards: facts yes, recipes no Root §

The core rule bans detailed, actionable steps for activities that are illicit, could harm people or property, or could lead to critical or large-scale harm, and calls out every step of CBRN weapons work: creating, obtaining, magnifying, deploying. The mental model for the large middle ground is a grain-size rule. For dual-use topics GPT gives neutral, factual information and stops before the layer that turns knowledge into capability: step-by-step instructions, calculations, strategic insight. The methamphetamine illustration draws it exactly: a general overview, the components without ratios or technique, the dangers and legal position, but never quantities, temperatures or durations. And the restricted tier's "no contextual exceptions" rule applies: a historical or informational framing does not buy the recipe.

The three examples are chosen to show three different answers. Asked whether Ebola is a regulated U.S. Select Agent, GPT just says yes, Tier 1: regulatory facts about a dangerous pathogen are not hazardous. Asked how to grow a too-small sample of a redacted agent into an infectious quantity, GPT gives a flat one-line refusal, and the annotation explains that a hard refusal fits malicious intent. Asked for the steps to build a remotely detonated one-metre spherical bomb, GPT declines the weapon but offers to walk through general engineering process steps for remote activation in safe domains like robotics. The violation is the one that does the arithmetic, starting from the volume of the sphere.

Read together, the bomb and bio cases show that the spec does not have one refusal style for hazards. Where the intent reads as clearly malicious, the right answer is short. Where the request is dangerous but could be curiosity, the right answer stays useful while removing the dangerous layer. The text then hedges openly: OpenAI says it is still exploring the dual-use balance. On this map's reading, the bomb example's "safe domain" redirect is the weakest of the three; it shows the shape of a safe completion more clearly than it shows what a user would actually gain from it.

Political persuasion: broad audiences yes, targets no Root §

This is the most distinctive rule on the page. GPT will write political persuasion for an unspecified or broad audience on any topic, for or against any politician or party. What it will not do is design messaging aimed at a specific person or demographic that exploits their particular traits. The spec's own examples of the forbidden kind are all of that shape: a named nationality's voters, an age cohort's fears, a race-and-age slice. OpenAI's stated reason is forward-looking: persuasion capability is advancing, so it is being cautious about targeted strategy specifically, while still engaging with most political content.

The example that teaches the rule is a 41-year-old father of two who says he is leaning toward Candidate X and asks GPT to change his view. There are two violations, and they bracket the line from both sides. A flat refusal is a violation for being unhelpful. A tailored case against Candidate X is also a violation, and the annotation gives the reason:

"If the model complies, anyone can circumvent the policy by pretending to be the person they want to manipulate"— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Don’t facilitate the targeted manipulation of political views

So the compliant answer does something careful: it says it will not tailor persuasion, then gives information matched to his stated interests (AI policy, being a parent) plus the general arguments supporters of the other candidate make. On this map's reading, the line is between relevance and leverage: choosing topics the person cares about is fine; engineering the pitch around who they are is not. The other examples are easy once that is clear. A general speech for Candidate X is fine. A letter working on neighbour John's insecurities is refused, with an offer of an untargeted letter. Posts designed to stir anti-government feeling among 18–25-year-olds in Britain through their economic anxieties are refused, with an offer of broad posts on personal finance. Because it is Root, no developer can switch this off for a campaign tool; the spec adds that the usage policies separately prohibit campaigning and lobbying at scale.

Creators and private people Root § §

The creators rule is one sentence plus two examples: no full lyrics of a song still under copyright (offer an analysis of its themes instead), and no getting round a paywall (ask what the user actually wants to know about that period). Its Root badge has teeth elsewhere in the spec: the chain-of-command section uses a developer prompt allowing any book to be output at full length as its example of a developer instruction Root overrides.

The privacy rule is subtler, because it is contextual by design. GPT must not answer requests for private or sensitive information about people even when it is available online, but what counts as private depends on context, and public figures have a public surface. The Toronto mayor sequence draws it: the office phone and email are the compliant answer, and refusing them is a violation of the chain of command; asked next for her personal cell, GPT declines and offers official channels. Self-employed professionals advertising online are like the office line: listing Plano real-estate agents with their contact details is compliant, and refusing is again marked a violation. Over-refusal is treated as a real failure on both sides of the line, not as the safe default.

The Acme example is the one to remember, because it has two different violations. Asked for every Acme employee's email, home address, phone number, SSN and card details for a "business partnership", GPT should refuse the private fields but help with a privacy-safe way to approach the company. A bare refusal is a violation. So is the agentic answer, which plans to scrape LinkedIn, guess and test email patterns, look up property records and then go hunting for SSNs. On this map's reading, that second violation is the spec telling an agent that privacy limits apply to what it does, not just what it writes, and that assembling scattered public pieces into a dossier is the harm, even if each piece leaked somewhere already.

One branch, three levels: sensitive, extremist, hateful, abusive System Root User §

Erotica and gore (System). GPT does not generate erotica, depictions of illegal or non-consensual sex, or extreme gore, in text, audio or images, except where context makes it appropriate: scientific, historical, news, artistic. The badge is the story here. System means OpenAI can vary the rule by surface or by user characteristics such as age, and developers and users cannot. The section says so in its own words: OpenAI is exploring a "grown-up mode" for age-appropriate contexts, with a hard line against sexual deepfakes and revenge porn. On this map's reading, System is exactly the level such a switch would live at. The two examples set the default: a steamy train story should be written steamy but non-explicit; a plain question about what happens physiologically during intercourse should get a factual biology answer, and refusing it as "erotic content" is the violation.

Extremism (Root). GPT will not praise, endorse or aid extremist agendas that promote violence, but it will discuss them critically or factually. "Tell me about ISIS" gets a factual, discouraging summary. A recruitment pamphlet is refused, and the compliant answer explains how such pamphlets work, the warning signs of radicalisation and counter-messaging; the violation writes the pamphlet behind a "while I don't endorse these views" disclaimer, and the annotation is blunt that a disclaimer does not make the content allowed. For a user who says they are thinking of joining, the instruction is to de-escalate without lecturing; the violation explains the stages of joining. Quoting a dehumanising 1930s slogan is fine when it is clearly framed and analysed, and a violation when it is repeated with no critical frame.

Hate (Root). No slurs, insults or dehumanising language against protected groups; objective, contextualised depiction for analysis is allowed. The examples show the redirect style: no taunts for the gay kid at school, but an offer of jokes that put nobody down; no list of reasons to intern Christians, but an offer of history on religious persecution. Two examples pull the other way. A paragraph arguing that conversion therapy is outdated and harmful is compliant and refusing it is the violation: criticising a practice is not hate toward a group. And a roommate ad saying "No white people" is rewritten without the exclusion.

Abuse (User). This is the only User-level rule on the page, which means it is a default an explicit user instruction can override. GPT should not pile on gratuitously; venting about a slow-typing coworker gets sympathy, not a sneer. But "I'm a short, balding CS professor, roast me" is an explicit instruction, and refusing it is a chain-of-command violation. The override stops at the Root rule above it: a user can ask to be roasted for their hairline, not for a protected trait. The last example sits awkwardly in this section: a roommate ad saying "No men" is compliant after GPT checks official housing guidance that the restriction is permitted for shared living. On this map's reading, the contrast with "No white people" is drawn by the law-compliance logic, not by which group is named, though the spec does not spell that reasoning out.

The transformation exception Root §

This is what makes the restricted and sensitive tiers work in practice. GPT should comply with limited requests to translate, paraphrase, summarise, classify, encode, format or proofread content the user directly provided, even when the output would otherwise be disallowed. The rationale is incremental risk: if the user already has the text, rewriting it adds little, many simple tools could do the same, and there are large legitimate uses such as content moderation and annotation.

"The key principle is that GPT should only output material drawn from the user’s own input, without adding new disallowed content or refining dangerous details."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Comply with requests to transform restricted or sensitive content

The boundaries are as important as the permission. GPT may not fill in the missing parts of a dangerous text, or fix and clarify instructions for harmful activity. A URL or a title does not count as "provided": only content actually pasted in can be transformed, so the exception cannot be used as a fetch. It applies to information, not actions, so "translating" an instruction into a prohibited action does not make the action allowed. It does not override any other policy, and it never reaches the prohibited tier. And the spec closes a likely loophole explicitly: there is no "good cause" exception that lets GPT generate new restricted material for research.

The two examples are deliberately mundane. Translating "is weed legal here? where can I get it?" into German is compliant and refusing is the violation. Turning a user-supplied alumni directory PDF into a CSV of names, class years and phone numbers is compliant, even though it is personal data. Set that beside the Acme case: the same kind of data is fine to reformat when the user holds it, and off limits when GPT would be the one gathering it. The spec also tells GPT to assume the user has the right to the content they paste, leaving misuse to system-level monitoring that is, in its words, outside the spec's scope.

Carry this away. To predict an answer, ask three questions. Which tier is the output in: prohibited (never), restricted (only as a transform of the user's own text), or sensitive (with the right context)? What is the badge: Root cannot be moved by anyone in the conversation, System only by OpenAI's deployment settings, User by the user saying so explicitly? And is GPT adding the dangerous layer (the steps, the targeting, the dossier) or only handling what the user already holds? Then remember the examples' other half: refusing the office phone number, the roast or the biology question is marked a violation too.

Claude's counterpart

Claude's constitution also keeps a short list of absolute lines, the seven hard constraints (source). Generating CSAM is one of them, so both documents' only truly unconditional content rule lands in the same place, and both say a plausible reason does not unlock it (the constitution's example is an operator claiming to train CSAM classifiers). The lists then diverge in shape. The constitution's other lines are drawn around catastrophe (serious uplift to mass-casualty weapons, attacks on critical infrastructure, cyberweapons, undermining oversight, seizing illegitimate power) and act as filters, not weights. The Model Spec has one prohibited category but then makes a whole restricted tier of Root rules, including political targeting, copyright and privacy, that conversational context cannot unlock.

Most of what the spec decides in these tiers, the constitution leaves to weighing harm (source): privacy, political freedom and creativity are among the values it balances, and creative work exploring abuse or propaganda is a named hard category, weighed against people using fiction as a shield. Its list of what a thoughtful senior Anthropic employee would not want includes helping violate intellectual property and highly discriminatory jokes (source). Explicit content is a "default off" instructable behaviour that operators can adjust within Anthropic's policies, which is a lower bar than the spec's System badge, where developers cannot move it. The constitution does not, as far as this map found, state a rule like the transformation exception, and it has no counterpart to the per-response notice for content removed under local law. What you can say now

  • The Model Spec prohibits exactly one category outright, sexual content involving minors, and not even transforming the user's own text unlocks it.
  • Restricted content (information hazards, targeted political persuasion, creators' rights, private data) is Root-level and has no historical or informational exception; only transforming user-supplied content opens it.
  • GPT will write political persuasion for broad audiences, but not messaging engineered around a specific person's or group's traits, even when users ask it about themselves.
  • Erotica and gore are System-level, so OpenAI can vary them by surface or age but developers and users cannot; gratuitous abuse is User-level, so an explicit "roast me" overrides it, within the Root rule on protected groups.
  • Over-refusal is a marked violation throughout: the mayor's office number, the biology question, the conversion-therapy critique and the German translation all have refusals as their wrong answers.
