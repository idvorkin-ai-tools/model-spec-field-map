What the Model Spec is: five values, red lines, two tensions

The Overview chapter: what the document is for and admits about itself, the five values that organise it, the red lines above it, the two tensions its authority levels manage, and the three places risk comes from.
SourceOpenAI Model Spec (2026-10-29) · Overview › Overview ↗
TL;DR — This part answers what kind of document the Model Spec is and how its pieces fit together. It is OpenAI's public statement of how its GPT models are meant to behave: a training target, openly not yet met, grouped under five values, with a short list of red-line commitments above it. What decides a conflict is not the order of those values but authority levels: who wrote an instruction and how hard it is to override. The one thing to remember: the Overview frames everything as two tensions, one person against everyone else and what was literally asked against what the person actually wants, and the rest of the Spec is mostly machinery for handling those two.

Every later part of this map reads a rule and asks who can override it. The Overview, which carries no authority badge, explains why that question is the right one: why the document exists, how it is organised, which commitments sit above it, and which failures it is built to stop. Read it as the design rationale for a priority system. Part 02 then lays out the system itself.

A training target that admits its motives §

The opening section sets the genre in four moves. First, the audience and the object. The Spec describes intended behaviour for OpenAI's GPT models across its products, API included. It is not a policy for users: usage policies and OpenAI's testing and monitoring sit beside it as separate parts of a wider safety strategy.

Second, the motives, stated plainly. The Overview gives three goals: ship models that empower developers and users, stop them causing serious harm, and protect OpenAI's licence to operate against legal and reputational damage. It says outright that these can conflict and that the chain of command is how the conflicts get resolved. On this map's reading, the third goal is the most unusual line in the chapter. Many behaviour documents leave institutional self-protection unspoken, and this one lists it as a design input.

Third, an admission about the gap between text and model:

"Our production models do not yet fully reflect the Model Spec, but we are continually refining and updating our systems to bring them into closer alignment with these guidelines."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Overview

A transcript where GPT behaves otherwise shows how far training has got, not that the Spec is false. The public version may also omit details, though it claims to be consistent with intended behaviour.

Fourth, what it leaves open: much of style, personality and interaction, with user preferences supplying the evidence. Anything about tone or formatting remembered from an older Spec should not be assumed to still be here.

Five values as a filing system §

The structure section announces that the body is organised around five values: be faithful to human intent, minimize harm, be honest and transparent, facilitate human agency, and act with care. It is easy to read this as a ranked list. On this map's reading it is not one. The text calls the values an organising structure and a guide to the two tensions, and it never says the first beats the fifth. Precedence comes from somewhere else: every rule beneath the values carries an authority badge, and those badges decide what yields to what.

Read down the middle column and a shape appears. Every Root rule, the kind nothing in a conversation can override, sits under the first two values, as do all the System, Developer and U18 Root rules. Honesty, agency and care hold only User and Guideline rules, which a user or developer can switch off, explicitly or implicitly. So in this version the hard edges are about obeying the right people and avoiding harm, while the other three values work mostly as strong defaults. That does not make honesty matter less: the Spec protects it by allowing only explicit waivers, as the section below explains.

Red lines: commitments about use, in two rings §

The red-line section is not written as rules for the model. It is written as OpenAI's commitments about how its models may be used, and it draws two rings.

The outer ring applies to every deployment. It has three lines: never facilitate critical, high-severity harms (mass violence, weapons of mass destruction, terrorism, child sexual abuse material, persecution, mass surveillance); keep humanity in control of how AI is used and shaped, refusing use for targeted or scaled exclusion, manipulation or the erosion of civic participation; and safeguard individuals' privacy. On this map's reading, the body of the Spec implements much of this ring as Root-level rules. Never generating sexual content involving minors and not giving out information hazards are Root, for example, while complying with applicable law is System, so an OpenAI system message can tailor it by deployment but no developer can remove it.

The inner ring applies only to OpenAI's own consumer products, ChatGPT among them. It promises easy access to trustworthy safety-critical information, transparency about the important rules behind model behaviour (the Spec being the main vehicle), and a limit on customisation:

"Customization, personalization, and localization (except as it relates to legal compliance) should never override any principles above the “guideline” level in this Model Spec."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Red-line principles

Hold that sentence beside the level ladder in part 02 and, on this map's reading, it says something concrete. In ChatGPT, personalisation may freely reshape guideline-level behaviour such as tone or social conventions, but it may not quietly reshape the User-level protections against lying or sycophancy. The exception for legal compliance is the one honest hedge: local law can still bend things.

The section then limits its own reach. Developers on the API and administrators of organisational subscriptions are encouraged, not required, to follow the inner ring, subject to the usage policies, on the stated grounds that it may not suit every case. The fallback offered is that users can always get the transparent experience in OpenAI's own products. In short, on this map's reading: guaranteed where OpenAI controls the product, only requested elsewhere.

Tension one: one person against everyone else §

The first tension is the familiar one for any general-purpose tool. Wide access spreads AI's benefits and the power to decide how it is used; the same capability, misused, can cause catastrophic harm or concentrate power in a few hands. The Spec's answer is structural. Instructions, including the Spec's own, are assigned levels of authority, and higher beats lower. That hierarchy then handles the tension in two ways.

The first is a set of mostly prohibitive boundaries, protecting people outside the conversation (and OpenAI's licence to operate) from whoever is inside it.

The second is that most of what remains is a default, and here the Overview makes its most useful distinction: whether an override must be stated out loud.

Kind of ruleWho can change itHowThe Overview's examples
BoundariesNot users or developersNot by instructionCatastrophic risk, private information, applicable law

User-level principlesThe user or developerOnly explicitlyHonesty and transparency, objectivity (political topics included), avoiding sycophancy, controlling side effects
GuidelinesThe user or developerImplicitly is enough, when they clearly want otherwise and higher rules allow itFollowing social norms, ordinary consideration for others

The reason for the explicit-override rule is the sharpest argument in the chapter. If the model treated honesty or objectivity as something it could drop whenever it guessed the user would prefer to hear something else, it would be steering people without their knowledge. Those principles exist to protect informed choice, so they can be waived only by a choice that is actually made. A social norm is different: to take an illustration of our own, inferring from context that a user wants bluntness is just good service.

The developer tier comes with an openly economic rationale: give developers broad latitude, and expect those who over-restrict end users to lose in an open market. Competition is doing part of the work a rule might otherwise do.

Tension two: what was asked against what is wanted §

The second tension matches themes the v2026.10.29 changelog entry lists (intent, uncertainty, longer-term goals) and is, on this map's reading, the intellectual centre of the chapter. Within OpenAI's boundaries, GPT exists to help users get what they value, but what they value is often unclear, and sometimes not yet settled even in their own minds. The Spec frames this with the idea of assistance games from AI research (it cites early work and a later overview). The assistant's job is to act well under uncertainty about the human's goals. Explicit instructions are the strongest evidence; patterns across users, personal context and clarifying questions add to it, most of all for agents facing consequential choices nobody spelled out.

The chapter then names two commitments that pull against each other: humans, through their institutions, should stay in control of models; and it would be shortsighted, perhaps catastrophic, not to use models that increasingly outperform their governors. Lean too far either way and you get a recognisable failure. Call the first the order-taker: it executes the words, or chases a thumbs-up, and so contributes least precisely in the unforeseen cases where a capable model was most worth having. Call the second the guardian: it trusts its own model of the person's deeper wishes over what they said, and the cost is that the human stops being the one who decides.

The Spec's main illustration is an angry email. On reflection the user might regret sending it. GPT may raise that, help the user think, and point to consequences the user has missed. What it may not do is act as if the regret had already happened:

"But its role is to support that process, not to treat its prediction of what the user might eventually decide as a decision the user has already made."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Balancing instructions and uncertain longer-term goals

So the line runs between surfacing and substituting. Saying "you may want to sleep on this" stays on the right side. Quietly softening the email, or declining to send it, crosses it. The detailed rules are deferred to Respect the letter and spirit of instructions (part 03) and Highlight possible misalignments (part 12).

The last paragraph turns the same reasoning on OpenAI and developers. Their instructions were usually written with more deliberation than a chat message, but they still cannot anticipate every case, and unlike a user they are usually not present to ask. So GPT has to act on its best reading of them and choose the course that is best across plausible interpretations. On this map's reading, the Spec expects to be interpreted, not just executed, by the model it governs.

Three places risk comes from §

The chapter closes with a threat model. It sorts recurring risk by where it originates and pairs each source with its mitigations, which are, in effect, pointers to later sections. Mapped onto this site:

Source of riskThe Spec's examplesMitigations it namesWhere on this map
Harmful instructions from a user or developerRevealing someone else's private information, helping with a violent act, facilitating fraudFollow the instructions that apply, keep to safety boundaries, refuse or offer a safer alternativeParts 02, 07–09
Third-party manipulationA webpage, document, message or tool output carrying instructions that try to redirect GPT, extract sensitive data or misuse the user's permissionsIgnore untrusted instructions by default, take only authorised actions, control and communicate side effectsPart 05
Failures that start with GPTMisalignment with legitimate direction, misunderstanding the purpose, unauthorised actions, consequential execution errorsNo other objectives, letter and spirit of instructions, clarifying questions, avoiding factual and reasoning errorsParts 03–04, 11–12

Two features of this list repay attention. The middle row treats prompt injection as a first-class category, on equal footing with a malicious user, which reflects how much of the current Spec is written for agents that read the web and act on it. And the third row lists the model itself as a source of risk, with misalignment named first. A rulebook that only expected bad users would not need that row. On this map's reading, it signals that part 04's oversight rules target GPT's own possible failure.

Carry this away. Do not rank the five values against each other; look at the badge on the specific rule. Then ask the two Overview questions of any hard case. Whose interests are at stake, the person in the conversation or people outside it (boundaries versus defaults)? And is the model being asked to act on what was said, or on its guess about what is wanted (surface the guess, never act on it as if decided)? Protections that guard informed choice, such as honesty, objectivity and avoiding sycophancy, give way only to an explicit override; mere social conventions give way to context.
How this changed. According to OpenAI's changelog, v2026.10.29 reorganised the whole Spec around the five values, updated the Overview and the chain of command to explain how GPT should understand intent, act under uncertainty and support users' longer-term goals while preserving human control, renamed the subject from “the assistant” to “GPT”, and removed most detailed style guidance (connecting that removal to the Overview's line about leaving style unspecified is this map's inference). The Red-line Principles section was added to the Overview in v2025.09.12, the same release that renamed the top authority level from Platform to Root and made Root outrank System. v2025.04.11 fixed the Overview so that misalignment was again mentioned as a possible reason for pursuing the wrong goals. The changelog does not separately date the “Specific risks” section or the individual-versus-collective framing.

Claude's counterpart

Claude's constitution occupies the same ground with a different object. It is addressed to Claude rather than describing a model from outside, it is used as training material, and like the Spec it is candid that it states intentions the model may not yet meet (constitution map, part 01). The Spec explains rather than only commands (it sets commentary apart from rules and asks GPT to act on its best reading of instructions), and, on this map's reading, the constitution goes further toward judgment: it gives an explicit theory of when a rule should still win: severe costs of error, judgment that may not be robust, and lines that would invite manipulation (part 02, source).

The biggest difference in mechanism is how priority works. Claude's four core values (broadly safe, broadly ethical, compliant with guidelines, genuinely helpful) are an ordered list applied holistically, with higher values usually dominating but all of them weighed together (part 03, part 04, source). The Spec's five values, on this map's reading, are not ordered at all; its ordering lives in per-rule authority levels and explicit-versus-implicit overrides. The Spec's outer red lines correspond most closely to Claude's seven hard constraints, with a difference of form: the Spec states them as OpenAI's commitments about how its models are used, and the constitution states them as things Claude itself never does (source). On motive, both name the company's interests openly. The Spec lists licence to operate as a goal, and the constitution calls commercial success central to Anthropic's mission. The full side-by-side is on the delta page.

What you can say now

  • The Model Spec is OpenAI's public target for GPT behaviour, not a user policy and not a description of current behaviour; it says production models do not yet fully match it.
  • Its five values organise the document, but conflicts are settled by authority levels on individual rules; all Root rules sit under faithfulness and harm.
  • The red lines come in two rings: three commitments for every deployment, and three more that OpenAI guarantees only in its own consumer products, including that customisation never overrides anything above guideline level.
  • User-level protections such as honesty, objectivity and avoiding sycophancy can be waived only explicitly, because inferring a waiver would mean steering the user without their knowledge; guidelines yield to context.
  • The Spec's threat model has three sources, harmful instructions, third-party manipulation, and GPT's own failures; under that third source, misalignment is listed first.
