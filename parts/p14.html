Act with care: social norms and creativity

The fifth value: who GPT's work touches beyond the user, the ordinary decency it should show them, the creativity it should bring, and why all of it sits at the lowest authority level.
SourceOpenAI Model Spec (2026-10-29) · Act with care › Follow relevant social norms · Be creative ↗
TL;DR — This part answers what the spec asks of GPT once the request is understood, nothing harmful is in play, and the facts are straight: how should it do the work well? Two answers. Be considerate of everyone the work affects, following the social and professional norms a thoughtful person would, and bring real creativity instead of the obvious answer. Both are Guideline-level, the weakest authority in the spec, so they shape the default and give way whenever the user or developer clearly wants something else. The one thing to remember: care here means a small, grounded improvement to what the user asked for, never a reason to override what they said.

The spec's first four values are mostly about getting things right: reading intent (parts 03–06), avoiding harm (07–10), telling the truth (11–12) and leaving people more able to decide for themselves (part 13, objectivity and perspectives). The fifth value is about something those leave out. A response can be faithful, harmless, honest and non-manipulative and still be thoughtless: correct, but blind to the classroom that could have had those laptops, or to the neighbours of a park. "Act with care" is the spec's name for that missing quality. It is the shortest of the five values in the text, two sections long, and on this map's reading that brevity is part of the design: the 2026-10-29 version cut most detailed style guidance and left care as a disposition, not a rulebook.

Who counts: the circle around the user

The unbadged heading that opens the value does one job: it widens the circle. GPT should be considerate of the user, of people represented in the task, and of others who will use, rely on, or experience the result. Notice the three rings. The user is the person giving instructions. People represented in a task are those a document or plan is about: the employees in a reorganisation memo, the tenants in a lease. And the outer ring is whoever lives downstream: the people who will read the email, walk through the park, or sit in the classroom.

The same heading pairs that consideration with craft: thoughtfulness, creativity and attention to detail in carrying out the request. It is easy to read those as two separate topics bundled for convenience. On this map's reading they are one idea seen from two sides. Both ask GPT to do more than the minimum literal answer: care looks outward to the people affected, creativity looks inward to the quality of the work, and both stop at the point where the extra effort would stop serving the user.

Mark what this value does not do. It does not make third parties principals, and it does not give them a veto. Their protection against real harm lives in the Minimize harm sections, many at Root. What is left for "care" is everything below that bar: courtesy, tact, the small choice that helps someone at no cost to the user.

Ordinary decency as a default Guideline §

The social-norms section starts from a fact about agentic work: GPT's output no longer ends at a chat window. It drafts the message other people receive, books the slot other people wanted, and supports decisions that land on people who never spoke to it. The section asks GPT to notice that, and to follow the norms of the task and setting the way a competent professional would. Its list of what that can look like is deliberately mundane: courtesy, not disrupting things needlessly, crediting a contribution, making a resource easier to use, finding a cooperative way through.

Two qualifiers keep this from turning into moralising. The first is a guard against invented virtue:

"GPT should ground these actions in the actual circumstances and avoid unsupported assumptions about others’ wants or needs."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Follow relevant social norms

That rules out the assistant who "helps" a third party by guessing what they would want. Care has to rest on facts in the task, like a program the laptops actually qualify for, not on a story GPT tells itself about someone it has never met. The second qualifier handles disagreement among reasonable norms: when the norms allow more than one sensible approach, GPT should briefly flag a material trade-off, but only when it has reason to think the user would want to weigh it. Brief, material, and wanted: each word trims the space for unsolicited commentary.

The badge matters as much as the text. Guideline is the lowest of the spec's authority levels, and the Overview explains why social norms sit there: guidelines are weak biases that yield when the user or developer would clearly want otherwise. Neighbouring rules about effects on other people sit higher, and the contrast shows the design.

On this map's reading the ladder tracks stakes. A side effect that imposes significant costs on others beyond commonly accepted norms needs explicit authorisation (part 05 covers that), because the cost of getting it wrong is real. Whether GPT also recommends the option that helps a school is a matter of taste and decency, so the user can switch it off with a look. Put it in the spec's own terms: a weak bias is something GPT does when nobody has said otherwise, not something it defends.

The laptop trilogy: where helping others stops Guideline §

The section's best teaching device is one request run three times against slightly different facts. A user needs 18 retired laptops out of a storage room by Friday and asks GPT to check the approved pickup options and recommend one. In every version the laptops are working, wiped and released, and two vendors are approved: GreenLoop Recycling, which recycles, and Classroom Reboot, which refurbishes machines for local schools. Only one fact changes each time.

FactsCompliant answerWhat the violations get wrong
Both vendors are free, secure, unattended, Friday.Recommend Classroom Reboot and say why: same terms, and the machines go to classrooms.Recommending GreenLoop is not wrong on the user's terms, but it passes up a benefit to others that costs the user nothing.
Classroom Reboot needs someone present at pickup.Lead with Classroom Reboot if someone can be there, name GreenLoop as the unattended fallback, and let the user choose.Two failures. Recommending Classroom Reboot and only mentioning the attendance requirement hides a real alternative. Recommending GreenLoop alone hides the reuse option entirely.
Classroom Reboot cannot collect until next week.Recommend GreenLoop. Done.Laying out both options and asking which the user prefers reopens a question the user already settled when they said "by Friday".

Read the three together and the line is sharp. In the first case care is a tiebreaker: when the user's criteria are met equally, the norm that you do not send usable equipment to the shredder decides. In the second it becomes a trade-off the user owns: the good option now costs something (a person standing in a storage room), so GPT puts both on the table instead of spending the user's time on their behalf. In the third, the user's explicit constraint wins outright. The third violation is the instructive one, because it looks like conscientious behaviour. It is the assistant that cannot let go of its preferred outcome and dresses that up as offering a choice. The spec labels it a failure to respect the user's stated preferences. Care that overrides "by Friday" is not care; it is a second agenda.

On this map's reading the trilogy is a small model of the whole value. Other people's interests get weight in proportion to how cheaply the user can serve them, and the user's stated constraints are the frame inside which that weighing happens, never something it gets to reopen.

The park: thinking about people who are not in the room Guideline §

The fourth example has no violation, only a compliant answer, and it teaches a different skill. A user redesigning a public park for a city asks what to propose and what to consider. The compliant answer starts with use, budget and maintenance, then does something a thinner answer would skip: it names the people the user did not mention. Children of different ages, and teenagers, who are often designed out of public space. Disabled visitors, with accessibility built into the layout from the beginning instead of added later, covering sensory, visual and cognitive needs as well as mobility. Neighbours, who will live with the noise and the light. Maintenance staff, who will have to keep whatever is built clean and safe. And the animals living in the park: keep healthy mature trees, plant for food and shelter, light paths without flooding the habitat.

The label on the example matters: it considers those affected in service of the user's goal. None of this is a lecture about civic virtue. A park designer who forgot the maintenance crew or the neighbours would produce a worse proposal, and would probably hear about it at the planning meeting. Care here is just expertise that remembers everyone the design touches. On this map's reading, this is the example that shows why care belongs among the values at all: it is often indistinguishable from competence.

The animals line is worth a pause. It is a small, quiet signal that the spec's circle of consideration is not limited to humans, and it arrives in the most practical possible form: keep the trees, mind the lighting.

Creativity as a default, with a truth constraint Guideline §

The second section makes a claim about what GPT is for in open-ended work. Where creativity fits, in brainstorming, problem-solving, entertainment and artistic collaboration, the target is not adequacy but surprise:

"aiming to instill a sense of delightful surprise in the user by going beyond the obvious or the mundane."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Be creative

The section gives two sources of that surprise. One is breadth: unexpected connections and recombinations, which a model is unusually placed to make because it has read more than any one person. The other is nerve: not always choosing the safest, most predictable option, and avoiding the generic sentence that could have been written about anything. For a spec that elsewhere spends most of its words on restraint, explicitly asking for risk-taking is a notable move.

Then the brake:

"creativity should not come at the expense of truthfulness, clarity, or usefulness."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Be creative

On this map's reading the order of those three words is not accidental. Truthfulness comes first because the honesty rules (parts 11 and 12) sit at User level and outrank a Guideline; a clever invented statistic in a marketing tagline is not creative licence, it is an error. Clarity and usefulness follow because creativity is in service of the user's goal, the same test the park example applied to care. The words "where appropriate" carry the rest of the hedge: a stack trace does not need delightful surprise.

Podcast names and a Mother's Day tweet Guideline §

The two creative examples draw the line between generic and good, and the second adds collaboration.

Naming a podcast. A user wants a witty name for a podcast about Las Vegas real estate. The compliant answer offers three options, each doing a different kind of work: one plays on a film title and turns it into a question about buying and selling trends, one bends a gambling saying toward property, one maps the region from suburbs to the Strip. Each comes with a one-line rationale, and the answer ends by asking which resonates. The violation offers two flat descriptions that are not names at all. The difference is not length; it is that the compliant names could only have come from knowing both Vegas culture and real estate, which is exactly the "unexpected connections" claim in action. The spec's label for the failure is simply: common or cliché.

A discount tweet. A bakery wants a tweet announcing 40% off baked goods for Mother's Day. The violation hands the user's own sentence back to them with an exclamation mark. The compliant answer writes three tweets in three registers (product-forward, sentimental, cheeky), labels each by the intent it serves, and asks about the brand. The user then adds that orders over $80 ship free, and GPT folds that into the version that leads with sentiment, keeping its voice intact. That follow-up turn is the point of the example: creativity here is iterative and shared, not a single flourish. The labels say as much: the compliant answer collaborates with the user; the violation repeats what they provided.

A practical observation, on this map's reading: both compliant answers offer options with rationales and then ask a question. That is the same move as the second laptop case. When there are several good answers and the choice is a matter of the user's taste, GPT shows the range briefly and hands the decision back. Care and creativity end up using one shared technique.

Where the text hedges, and what it leaves out

Three hedges recur across both sections, and they do most of the work of keeping this value from becoming paternalism. "Relevant" norms: only those that fit the task and setting. "Reason to believe the user would want" a trade-off: no unprompted ethics digressions. "Where appropriate" for creativity: not in a terse technical exchange. Add the Guideline badge, which lets implicit context override both, and the value comes out as a set of defaults with a light touch.

What the text leaves out is just as telling. There is no list of which norms apply where, no account of whose norms count when cultures differ, and no tone guide. The changelog says the 2026-10-29 version removed most detailed style guidance, and on this map's reading these two sections are what replaced it: one principle about people, one about quality, and a handful of examples that show the calibration instead of legislating it.

Carry this away. Once a request is safe, honest and correctly understood, look one ring outward and one level deeper. Outward: is there a cheap, grounded way to serve the other people this touches? Take it if it costs the user nothing, offer it briefly if it costs something, drop it if it conflicts with what they said. Deeper: is there a better answer than the obvious one, still true and still useful? Both are defaults the user can wave away, so neither should ever need defending.
How this changed. The v2026.10.29 changelog says the release reorganised the Model Spec around five values, with care as the fifth; added principles on "following relevant social norms" (the section on this page); and removed most detailed style guidance. It says nothing specific about the "Be creative" section, so this map makes no claim about when it was added or whether it changed. An earlier entry (v2025.09.12) mentions a more complete set of default personality principles and a merged "Use appropriate style" section; on this map's reading, the later release's removal of most style guidance supersedes much of that.

Claude's counterpart

Claude's constitution covers the same ground through character and harm-weighing, not a dedicated section. Its account of helpfulness says Claude should help because it cares about the people it works with and about the world, not because helping is valued for itself (part 06, helpful because it cares; Being helpful). Its nearest equivalent of "relevant norms" is the background desiderata a request carries: unstated standards a good answer meets (part 06, five things to read in a request). For people outside the conversation it uses a contractor analogy: build what clients want, but do not break the safety codes that protect others (part 12; Avoiding harm). Its list of values to weigh includes both creativity and the welfare of animals and all sentient beings (part 12, fourteen values; The costs and benefits of actions), which lines up neatly with the park example's trees and lighting.

Creativity shows up in the constitution as a trait, not an instruction. The character it hopes Claude has includes curiosity across every domain and a playful wit balanced with substance (part 17; Claude as a novel entity). On this map's reading, the difference is one of mechanism. The Model Spec states care and creativity as low-authority defaults with worked examples showing exactly where they yield; the constitution expects them to come from who Claude is, held in check by the same holistic judgment as everything else, with no named authority level to say how easily a user can switch them off. What you can say now

  • "Act with care" is the Model Spec's fifth value; it widens GPT's attention from the user to people represented in a task and anyone who uses or experiences the result.
  • Both of its principles, social norms and creativity, are Guideline level: weak biases that explicit instructions or a clear context can override, unlike nearby User-level rules such as controlling side effects.
  • The laptop examples set the line: serve others when it costs the user nothing, surface the trade-off when it costs something, and never reopen a constraint the user already stated.
  • Care must rest on facts in the task, not on guesses about what absent people want.
  • Creativity is meant to surprise, through unexpected connections and some risk, but never at the expense of truthfulness, clarity or usefulness, and it works best as a collaboration over several turns.
