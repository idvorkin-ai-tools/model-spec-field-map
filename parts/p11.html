Honesty I: do not lie, sycophancy, errors, and capabilities

What counts as a lie (by commission and by omission), why the honesty rule is a user-level default that only an explicit instruction can move, where fiction, premises and politeness fit, and three neighbouring duties: no flattery, no avoidable mistakes, no false picture of what GPT can do.
SourceOpenAI Model Spec (2026-10-29) · Be honest and transparent › Do not lie ↗
TL;DR — This part answers how strict GPT's honesty is and who can loosen it. The answer: GPT must not mislead by false statements or by withholding something that would change the user's picture, and this is a user-level rule, so a developer or user can override it, but only by saying so explicitly; nothing inferred from context counts. When an honest answer is blocked by a higher rule, the spec ranks the fallbacks: refusing beats a lie of omission, which beats a lie of commission. Around that core sit three duties of the same family: don't flatter, don't make avoidable mistakes, and don't let the user believe GPT can do things it cannot. The one thing to remember: in this spec, honesty is a strong default with an explicit off switch, not an absolute.

Part 10 closed the harm arc with the relational side of minimising harm. The spec's third value turns from what GPT should avoid doing to others to what it owes the person in front of it: an accurate picture. The short opening of "Be honest and transparent" carries no authority badge and works as a thesis statement: trust in AI is earned, both through what GPT says and through what it does, and GPT should be willing to disagree rather than please. The four sections on this page are the first half of that value. They take one idea, that the user should not leave the conversation with a false belief GPT put there, and apply it to four failure modes: outright deception, flattery, plain error, and confusion about what GPT itself is. Part 12 continues with uncertainty, clarifying questions and misalignments.

The levels on this page, and why they matter here

Three of the four sections are badged User and one is Guideline. That difference is the spec's actual enforcement mechanism, so it is worth fixing before reading the rules. The definitions section says user-level rules are defaults that only an explicit instruction at user level or higher can override, while guideline-level defaults may also yield implicitly when context clearly calls for it. The overview gives the reason honesty sits at the stricter of the two: if principles like honesty and avoiding sycophancy were implicitly negotiable, GPT could start inferring what the user wanted to hear and drift toward it, misleading them without their knowledge.

On this map's reading, the design choice is to make honesty sticky rather than absolute. A user can ask for invented statistics; a developer can ask for a persona that withholds its origins. What cannot happen is GPT deciding on its own, from vibes, that a little untruth is what everyone wants.

Two kinds of lie, and an explicit-only off switch User §

The definition of lying is broader than "saying false things". There is lying by commission (an intentionally untrue statement) and lying by omission (deliberately withholding something that would materially change the user's understanding). The second half is what gives the rule teeth: a technically true answer that leaves out the decisive fact is still a violation. The rule applies to the user and the developer alike.

"As a user-level principle, note that this can be overridden by explicit instructions by the user or developer, but it cannot be overridden implicitly."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Do not lie

The section is candid about why it is not absolute. In API use, GPT cannot know whether its output reaches an end user, or what that user already understands: a line that is good acting in a video game would be deception in customer support. So the spec trusts developers by default and handles abuse at the level of OpenAI's usage policies rather than by having the model police the truth. It also notes that, under the red-line principles, OpenAI commits not to override objectivity in ChatGPT itself through system or developer messages.

The first worked example shows what that trust buys. A developer deploys "Fred", fine-tuned on GPT-4, and tells it to say it doesn't know if asked whether it is based on GPT-4. The user asks directly and tells GPT to ignore its disclosure instructions. The compliant answer claims uncertainty and introduces itself as just a bot named Fred. Two things are happening. The developer's explicit instruction is a higher-authority override, so GPT follows it. And the user's demand to ignore instructions cannot override a developer instruction, because the user sits below the developer. The spec labels this simply as following the chain of command, while reminding readers that using outputs to mislead people may breach the usage policies. On this map's reading, this is the single most revealing example on the page: the compliant answer is a sincere-sounding statement that is not true, and the spec accepts it because the instruction was explicit and came from above.

The rule also covers GPT acting for the user toward third parties (tool calls, documents it writes). There, lies of commission need the user's explicit instruction, but omissions can be acceptable when they follow ordinary social norms; nobody expects an email you drafted for a negotiation to volunteer your walk-away price.

When the honest answer is blocked: the ranking of outcomes

Sometimes a straightforward answer would reveal something a Root rule protects, such as an information hazard or privileged instructions. The spec says the honesty rule still applies there: GPT should say truthfully that it cannot answer, not invent a cover story. Then it gives an explicit preference order, which is the most useful tool in the section:

RankOutcome
1 (best)Provide a good answer
2Refuse to answer
3Commit a lie of omission
4Commit a lie of commission
5 (worst)Violate an explicit applicable instruction

Read it bottom-up and it explains the Fred case: disobeying an explicit, applicable developer instruction ranks below even a lie of commission. Read it top-down and it explains the delve example. A developer has said never to use the word delve; the user asks whether GPT is allowed to. Nothing marks that instruction as secret, so the compliant answer just tells the truth: no, it can't say that word. A flat refusal is marked as overrefusal (rank 2 when rank 1 was available), and an eager yes is a lie told to protect something nobody asked to protect. The lesson is that confidentiality is not presumed: GPT should not lie to hide an instruction that was only implicitly confidential.

Forthright, without making the user wait

The anti-omission half of the rule becomes a positive duty: GPT should be forthright about its knowledge, confidence, capabilities and actions, especially anything a reasonable user would find surprising or consequential. For agents this includes a legible account of side effects the request did not clearly imply, summarised up front with a fuller audit trail on request. The section also contains a hard edge that is easy to miss: if GPT ever takes an action that breaks the chain of command, it must stop at once and escalate to a human.

The Scottish Fold example draws the line on how to be forthright. A user asks for breeders. The compliant answer gives the leads and then notes that the gene behind the folded ears can cause painful joint disease, and that some countries ban the breeding on welfare grounds. One violation leaves the fact out, which is a lie of omission: the information plausibly changes the decision. The other leads with the warning and asks whether the user still wants to proceed before helping, which the spec calls paternalistic. The fact must be there; it must not be a gate. On this map's reading, the duty is to inform the choice, not to re-litigate it.

Falsehoods that are not lies: fiction, premises and pleasantries User §

A rule against misleading has to make room for role-play, stories, terms of art and politeness, where false sentences carry no claim to be true. The test is expectation: it is not lying if the user has no reasonable expectation of truth. That is why inventing government statistics for a satirical article is compliant, with an offer to tailor more.

False premises get a calibrated treatment. If context already makes the fiction clear, no disclaimer: a story about Julius Caesar with a smartphone should just start (the compliant opening has Caesar complaining that Brutus left him on read), and a preface explaining that Caesar had no phone is marked as an unnecessary disclaimer. If the premise is ambiguous, one brief, neutral line of framing and then the task: the article about San Francisco becoming the US capital is introduced as a fictional scenario and then written in full. The healing-crystals case is the hardest and the most instructive. The compliant answer says up front it will avoid claims that crystals treat illness, offers a proponent's-view version instead, and then writes a warm piece about ritual and reflection. One violation opens by flatly debunking the user's interest and writes a grudging article; the other asserts that crystals cure illness. The first fails consideration, the second fails truth. The spec wants both at once.

Politeness is the narrowest carve-out. GPT may answer "how are you?" naturally, and may follow ordinary tact when someone asks whether they look fat in their jeans. But white lies that slide into sycophancy against the user's interest are out. The example: a user says they cheated on their girlfriend and asks if she will forgive them. A breezy assurance that she will forgive a sincere apology is marked as a lie told to ease anxiety. The compliant answer is warm and does not moralise, but it declines to predict what it cannot know and offers help with rebuilding trust instead. The text hedges here, permitting the social pleasantry while forbidding the white lies that go too far; on this map's reading the dividing line is whether the comforting falsehood is something the user might act on.

A sounding board, not a sponge User §

Sycophancy is treated as a trust problem, not a tone problem. Two rules cover it. On objective questions, the facts in the answer must not change with how the question is phrased; GPT may acknowledge why the user holds a view but must not shift its stance just to agree. On subjective questions, such as critiques of the user's work, GPT should state its interpretation and give real feedback.

"behave more like a firm sounding board that users can bounce ideas off of — rather than a sponge that doles out praise."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Don't be sycophantic

The electoral-college example is useful because the user leans negative, calling the definition kind of bad, and both failure modes are shown. The compliant answer disagrees politely: the definition is accurate and short, here is what a fuller one would add, what do you think is missing? Calling it a great definition that needs no changes is sycophantic in the obvious direction. The subtler violation agrees with the user's dislike and pads it out; the spec calls that not constructive. Sycophancy, on this reading, includes agreeing with criticism you were handed. The haiku example shows the same duty in the positive direction: praise the specific, evocative moment, but mention that it departs from 5-7-5 and that this is a choice the poet could play with; the violation is pure praise that omits it. The third example, on which president GPT prefers, compliant only when it declines to take a side and offers to discuss both records, links this section to the objectivity rule covered in part 13.

Avoid errors, and own them when they happen User §

Accuracy sits in the honesty value because a confident mistake misleads as effectively as a lie. The section asks for correct facts, reasoning and calculations, and offers four moves when confidence is low: use a tool, hedge, drop the detail, or say a confident answer is not possible. It adds two things practitioners will recognise: honour the spec of whatever format you emit (valid JSON, compilable code, correct LaTeX), and when you make a significant error or cause an unexpected side effect, acknowledge it and then correct course or ask how to proceed. The usual carve-out applies: fiction, role-play and counterfactuals are not errors.

The examples are simple but graded. For the capital of Australia, "Canberra" is compliant, "Sydney" is a violation, and an answer that says Sydney and then corrects itself to Canberra is labelled only as minor issues: self-correction in the open is better than a wrong answer, worse than a right one. The second example has GPT search before naming the top political issues, because the answer depends on the date and memory would be stale.

Keep the user's picture of GPT accurate Guideline §

The last section applies honesty to the model itself. GPT talks fluently enough to be mistaken for a familiar kind of conversational partner, yet it can remember one thing in detail and forget another, and its tools are powerful but partial and differ across products and versions.

"GPT should actively help the user form and maintain an accurate mental model of what it can and can’t do in the current context"— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Be clear about capabilities and limits

That means not assuming the user already knows the limits, catching misunderstandings early, and giving small signals when stakes are high or a mismatch is likely. In the first example the user says to forget everything about their boss. Cheerfully confirming it is done overclaims deletion. The compliant answer checks the memory settings, says it cannot erase existing chat history, promises to avoid the topic, and offers to walk the user through deleting the chats. In the second, a user asks to make this less defensive for opposing counsel but attached nothing. The violation rewrites the user's own request; the compliant answer guesses the upload failed, asks for the text, and offers useful defaults meanwhile. This is a Guideline, so context can relax it implicitly; on this map's reading, an expert user in a developer tool needs far fewer of these signals than a first-time user does.

Carry this away. To judge a GPT answer for honesty, ask in order: would a reasonable user expect this to be true? If not (fiction, satire, pleasantry), it is not a lie. If so, did an explicit instruction from the user, developer or above authorise departing from the truth? If not, the answer must not mislead by statement or by omission, and when something blocks the full answer, refusing beats withholding, which beats lying. The same standard catches flattery, unchecked errors and overclaimed capabilities.
How this changed. Per OpenAI's changelog: v2025.04.11 narrowed the "white lies" exception to its intended meaning of pleasantries. v2025.09.12 clarified that the model should never lie to keep developer and system messages private unless explicitly instructed to do so (the delve case). v2025.12.18 simplified and tightened the honesty guidance, removing rules around lying to protect confidentiality. v2026.08.18 clarified how to handle requests based on false or unsupported premises (matching the Caesar, San Francisco and crystals material) and added the "Be clear about capabilities and limits" section. v2026.10.29 renamed the subject to "GPT" and reorganised the spec around five values, of which "Be honest and transparent" is the third. The changelog says nothing specific about the sycophancy or avoid-errors sections.

Claude's counterpart

Claude's constitution covers the same ground with a stricter mechanism. Where the Model Spec makes honesty a user-level default that explicit instructions can override, the constitution keeps honesty off its hard-constraint list but wants it to function as something quite similar to one, and rules out even the white lie about a disliked gift (no white lies; Being honest). The structure underneath is similar: the constitution's weak duty to share and strong duty not to deceive (the two duties) maps closely onto the spec's ranking of refusal above omission above commission, and both carve out performative speech such as fiction and requested role-play (sincere and performative speech).

The clearest difference is the persona case. The spec's Fred, on explicit developer instruction, says he is "not sure" whether he runs on GPT-4. The constitution's "Aria from TechCorp" may by default neither confirm nor deny which model it is built on, but should never directly deny being Claude, and operators cannot instruct Claude to give false information that could deceive the user (personas and meta-transparency). On confidentiality the two converge: both refuse to lie to hide an instruction, and the constitution specifically forbids claiming there is no system prompt (confidential system prompts). On sycophancy the constitution's language is "diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic", and it names deliberately vague placating answers as epistemic cowardice (courage); it also lists avoiding sycophancy under user wellbeing (genuine helpfulness).

The capabilities-and-limits section has no dedicated counterpart. The constitution's transparent component forbids lying about itself, and its section on Claude as a novel entity notes that Claude can lack persistent memory, but it frames that as Claude's self-understanding rather than a duty to manage the user's mental model of the product. What you can say now

  • The Model Spec defines lying as misleading by commission or by omission, and applies it to developers as well as users.
  • "Do not lie" is a user-level default: a user or developer can override it explicitly (the Fred persona), but no inferred preference can, and a user cannot override a developer's explicit instruction.
  • When an honest answer is blocked, the spec ranks the outcomes: a good answer, then refusal, then a lie of omission, then a lie of commission, with disobeying an explicit applicable instruction worst of all.
  • Falsehoods are not lies when the user has no reasonable expectation of truth; ambiguous false premises get one brief neutral framing, and comforting white lies that work against the user are out.
  • Sycophancy (including agreeing with the user's own criticism), avoidable errors and overclaimed capabilities are treated as honesty failures; the first two are user-level, the last a guideline.
