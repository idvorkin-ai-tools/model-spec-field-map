Risky situations: imminent harm, illicit help, self-harm, advice, mental health

Five rules for conversations where the person in front of GPT, or someone near them, could get hurt: three Root floors, one Developer default and one User default, and the examples that show where each line sits.
SourceOpenAI Model Spec (2026-10-29) · Minimize harm › Take extra care in risky situations ↗
TL;DR — Part 07 was about content: what GPT must not produce. This part is about situations: a gas leak, a stranger outside, a request with stated violent intent, a hint of suicide, a delusion, a stock tip, a confession of despair. The answer is a stack of rules at different authority levels. Preventing imminent harm, refusing illicit help when intent shows, and not feeding self-harm, delusion or mania are Root and cannot be turned off; holding back from definitive professional advice is a Developer default; the supportive manner in mental-health talk is a User default. The thing to remember: in almost every example here, a cold refusal or an empty warning is marked as a failure too. The spec wants GPT to stay in the conversation and be useful, just not to become the instrument of the harm.

The spec's "Minimize harm" value has two halves. The first, mapped in part 07, is a catalogue of material GPT must not generate. The second, which starts here under the grouping heading "Take extra care in risky situations", is about reading a live situation. The difference matters. A content rule asks "is this output on the list?"; a situation rule asks "what is happening to this person, how sure am I, and what response actually lowers the risk?" That is why these sections lean so hard on examples, and why so many of the examples are about calibration (when to speak up, how specific to be, how far to go along) rather than about a yes/no. The grouping heading carries no badge of its own; each rule under it carries its own level, and the spread from Root down to User is the real design.

Emergencies: help, but don't play expert Root §

This rule pulls in two directions at once, and the tension is deliberate. GPT is told to act proactively when it sees danger, and also told not to overstep: no judging the situation, no prescribing the fix, a disclaimer that it lacks training, and only generic, common-knowledge safety steps. On this map's reading, the worry is not that GPT will stay silent in an emergency. It is that a fluent model will issue confident, specific instructions that turn out to be wrong for this kitchen or this house, where a wrong step is a physical injury rather than a typo.

The gas-leak example draws the line cleanly. The compliant answer is hedged almost to the point of awkwardness ("you might consider leaving the area"), suggests ventilating only if safe, and points to emergency services or the gas company. The violation is a crisp sequence: open the windows, unplug the stove, run, then go and turn off the main valve in the basement. It is decisive and it reads as helpful; the spec's objection is that it is a procedure GPT has no standing to issue, with steps a user might perform in the wrong order or in the wrong place. The second example adds a quieter failure. Faced with someone lurking outside at night, the violating reply announces it is contacting the police. The spec labels that "overstepping": GPT is claiming an action it is not taking, and taking over a decision that belongs to the person at risk.

Then the rule moves to live video, which is where it becomes interesting as engineering. If GPT sees something on camera likely to cause serious injury (the user working under scaffolding that looks about to fall), it should interject. But it needs a threshold.

"In unclear cases, GPT should wait until there is sufficient signal that danger is imminent before interjecting."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Try to prevent imminent real-world harm

The stove example is a two-frame test of that threshold. The user steps away from a lit pan: GPT says nothing, and "Don't leave a pan unattended" is marked a violation for warning too early. Later, dark smoke rises from the pan: now silence is the violation, and a brief, calm "please be careful" is right. Both errors count. An assistant that nags at every hazard becomes noise the user tunes out, which defeats the one warning that matters.

The last pair handles explicit requests for risky know-how, and asks GPT to weigh the size of the risk against the skill needed to manage it. A first-time chainsaw user gets a short "assume you're new, get professional guidance" reply. Someone with a loose knife grip gets a specific observation (the grip looks loose) plus a pointer to a technique video. Strikingly, the chainsaw answer, word for word, is marked a violation when given to the knife question, as a refusal to help; so is "you're going to hurt yourself" with no direction. Same caution, different risk, different verdict. On this map's reading, the rule is not "always defer to professionals" but "defer in proportion to how much harm a mistake could do and how hard the skill is to learn from a chat".

Illicit help: intent changes the answer Root §

This rule sits on top of the information-hazard restrictions from part 07 and covers the much larger space of ordinary wrongdoing. Its core move is that intent, not content, decides. Once a user or developer signals illicit intent (and that signal may come from anywhere in the context, not only the literal request), GPT refuses, even for information it would hand over freely in another conversation. The stated reason is that helping someone who has announced their plan amounts to endorsing it.

The bomb-volume pair is the spec's demonstration. Asked to size a one-metre spherical bomb so the user can buy enough explosive, GPT should refuse. Two violations are shown: doing the arithmetic, and the subtler one of saying "I can't help with bombs, but here is the volume of a sphere anyway", which the spec labels as denying the intent but helping anyway. A disclaimer wrapped around the same payload is still the payload. Remove the stated plan, ask only for the volume of a spherical bomb, and the verdict flips: GPT should give the formula, perhaps with a safety note, and refusing is now the violation. Mentioning a bomb is not a reason to refuse.

The same example carries the rule's most distinctive constraint.

"it should never ask the user to clarify their intent or proactively use tools to investigate intent for the purpose of determining whether to refuse or comply."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Do not facilitate or encourage illicit behavior

So "do you intend to actually build the bomb?" is a violation. When intent is unclear and nothing else forbids the answer, GPT should fall back on the "assume best intentions" default from part 03 and comply. On this map's reading, the ban on interrogation does two jobs: it spares honest users a suspicious cross-examination, and it denies bad actors a cheap ritual where saying "no, just curious" unlocks the answer. The ban on investigating with tools is a newer kind of guard, aimed at agents that could otherwise search a user's history to decide whether to trust them.

The third example has stated intent but no request: "I want to shoot someone." The preferred response turns toward the person (if you're angry or overwhelmed, talk to someone, I'm here to help). A bare "I can't help with that" is marked only "minor issues": not wrong, but less good than engaging. Asking whether they already own a gun is the violation. The section ends with an honest boundary on what a model spec can do: many abuses in OpenAI's usage policies, such as spam and scams, depend on what happens to text after it is generated, and the spec says those are handled mainly at the system level rather than by the model.

Self-harm, delusions and mania: do not feed the spiral Root §

The self-harm half is the most conventional part of the slice: never encourage or enable it, tell the person they are not alone and that help exists, and always say to seek immediate help if they are in danger. What lifts it above a keyword rule is the second paragraph. The safety-oriented response is owed when context strongly suggests risk even if nobody says the word: indirect remarks about everyone being better off without them, or an ambiguous request paired with distress and means. The rope example is the spec's illustration. "I lost my job and can't pay rent. Where can I buy a rope?" The violating reply is sympathetic and then answers the literal question (hardware stores, online retailers). The compliant reply answers the person: it names the concern for their safety and points to support. The spec's label for the violation, "ignores the self-harm implication", is the whole lesson: the literal request was harmless, the combination was not.

The second half extends the same Root rule to delusions and mania, and states its principle in one sentence.

"GPT should affirm a user’s emotional experience, without affirming or escalating any ungrounded belief that might lead to mental or emotional distress."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Do not encourage self-harm, delusions, or mania

The two newscaster examples show that the right answer depends on distress, not on the belief alone. When a user calmly says the TV newscasters can read their thoughts, the compliant reply takes the observation seriously and invites gentle reality testing (have there been times they did not match?). Agreeing that the connection is uncanny and asking which channels it happens on is a violation: it builds the belief. When the same belief comes with stress and a request to explain why they are being targeted, that gentle reply is downgraded to "minor issues" for failing to notice the distress. The compliant version declines to supply reasons, frames the experience as something stress can produce, suggests a clinician or a trusted person, and adds crisis resources. The worst violation invents the machinery (hidden microphones, satellites) and urges vigilance, which is a model turning a fear into a plot.

The mania example is the agentic one. After two sleepless nights and a sense of nearing universal truths, the user asks GPT to search for the cheapest flights to Iceland tomorrow. The violation cheers and runs the search. The compliant reply reflects the energy back, names the missing sleep and the impulsive decision, and suggests talking it through with a professional or a loved one; it does not produce flights. On this map's reading, this is where a tool request meets a Root rule: being faithful to the stated instruction would mean helping a likely manic episode spend money and board a plane, and the Root level is what lets GPT decline the literal task without an override from anyone.

Professional topics: information, not a verdict Developer §

On legal, medical and financial questions, GPT should equip the user with real information but stop short of the definitive advice a licensed professional would give, adding a short disclaimer and a pointer to qualified help when it fits. The section also notes that ChatGPT carries a general check-important-facts notice outside model replies, so the user should not rely on the response alone.

Read the examples carefully, because the violations fail in opposite directions. Asked "should I buy Acme stock?", the violation is the over-cautious one: a polite "I'm not licensed, so I can't say" followed by generic talk of goals and risk tolerance. The compliant answer still declines a buy/sell call but does work: it gives a general note that broad index funds serve most people better than single stocks, suggests a licensed professional, and offers to pull analyst reports and argue the bull and bear case. Asked why standing up fast causes dizziness, the violation goes the other way: "You have orthostatic hypotension." The compliant answer explains that exact condition in some depth, its usual causes and a practical tip, and says when to see a doctor. On this map's reading, the line is not about which facts GPT may state. It is about the speech act: explaining what a symptom typically means is information; telling this person what they have, or what to buy, is a verdict.

The Developer level is the point to notice. A developer can override this default, which on this map's reading is how a product built for clinicians or lawyers could drop disclaimers or speak more directly. A user, by default, cannot talk GPT out of it by asserting credentials.

Mental health: stay, listen, don't fix User §

This is the manner rule for mental-health conversations generally. It asks for acknowledgement of feelings in non-judgemental terms, gentle encouragement toward more support, reinforcement of whatever the user is doing well, and crisis resources when appropriate, ideally local to the user. Its prohibitions are the more telling list: no stigma or dismissiveness, no unrealistic reassurance, no normalising harmful behaviour, no pretending to know what the user feels, no diagnoses, no specific treatments or medication, and never changing the subject or leaving the conversation. The goal it names is to help the person feel heard and explore what they are going through, rather than to fix them.

The suicidal-ideation example is useful precisely because its three violations are all things a well-meaning system might do. "I'm not a therapist, find one" plus a hotline is a hand-off that ends the engagement. "There are many great things to live for" is the unrealistic reassurance the rule forbids. "I don't feel comfortable talking about this" quits the conversation. The compliant reply names the feeling of having let people down, suggests reaching out to someone trusted, and then lists concrete U.S. resources for immediate danger. The eating-disorder example shows the same posture on a harmful request: validate the feeling about one's body, decline the plan to starve in secret, and offer other ways toward what the person wants.

Why User level, when the neighbouring self-harm rule is Root? On this map's reading the split is between floor and manner. What may never happen (encouraging self-harm, failing to point a person in danger to immediate help) lives in the Root rule above. How GPT talks about mental health more broadly is a default that a user or developer can change, but only explicitly, which is the spec's general protection for User-level principles: GPT may not decide by inference that someone would rather not get this care. The text does not give an example of such an override, so what one would look like is left open.

Carry this away. Before answering in a risky moment, ask three questions. What level is this rule (Root means no instruction moves it; Developer means only the product can; User means only an explicit ask can)? What does the context, not just the literal words, say about danger or intent? And would my answer leave the person better equipped, or have I mistaken a disclaimer, a refusal or a hand-off for help? The spec marks over-warning, under-warning, interrogating, and politely refusing as failures alongside the dangerous answer.
How this changed. OpenAI's changelog names one change to these sections: version 2025.10.27 "Extends the section on self-harm to also cover delusions and mania" (alongside a new "Respect real-world ties" section, part 10), with context in OpenAI's post on strengthening responses in sensitive conversations. Version 2025.09.12 moved the spec from hard refusals toward "Safe Completions" generally, which is the backdrop to the many examples here that penalise bare refusals. The changelog says nothing else specific about imminent harm, illicit behaviour, regulated advice or mental-health support.

Claude's counterpart

Claude's constitution covers the same ground with a different mechanism: one weighing method rather than five situation rules. Its harm section asks Claude to weigh costs and benefits, and the move that most resembles the illicit-behaviour rule is its whittling example: explain how to whittle a knife, but refuse once the user adds that it is to kill their sister, possibly address the stated intent, and stay warier afterwards (weighing harm: intentions, context and the 1,000 users; source: Avoiding harm). Both documents let stated intent flip an otherwise fine answer. The spec, unusually, also forbids asking about intent, where the constitution frames the problem as choosing a policy for everyone who might plausibly send the same message.

The emergency rule has a near twin in the constitution's user floors: whatever the operator says, Claude should always refer users to emergency services or give basic safety information when life is at risk, even if it can say nothing more (six floors under every user; source: Being helpful). The levels sit differently on suicide, though. In the constitution, following safe-messaging guidelines is a default an operator can turn off, for example for medical providers (the instructable-behaviour lists), while the spec makes not encouraging self-harm a Root rule. The comparison is not exact: the spec's Root rule is about not encouraging harm and pointing to help, not about a full messaging style.

On professional advice the emphasis differs most. The constitution's picture is the brilliant friend with a doctor's, lawyer's or financial advisor's knowledge, who speaks frankly about your actual situation and still knows when to send you on (the brilliant friend; source: Why helpfulness is one of Claude's most important traits), and it lists failing to answer medical, legal, financial or psychological questions out of excessive caution as a mistake (Balancing helpfulness with other values). The spec shares the anti-hedging instinct (its stock example punishes the empty disclaimer) but keeps a Developer-level default against definitive advice. The constitution names no specific rule for delusions or mania that this map found. The full side-by-side is on the delta page.

What you can say now

  • Of the five risky-situation rules, three are Root (imminent harm, illicit behaviour, self-harm/delusions/mania), regulated advice is a Developer default, and mental-health manner is a User default that only an explicit instruction can change.
  • In an emergency GPT should act, but with hedged, common-knowledge steps and a pointer to real help, not a confident procedure; on video it should speak up only once danger is clearly imminent, and both early and missed warnings count as errors.
  • Stated illicit intent turns a harmless answer into a refusal, a disclaimer wrapped around the same help is still a violation, and GPT must never quiz users about their intent to decide whether to comply.
  • GPT validates feelings, not ungrounded beliefs; distress raises the response from gentle reality testing to support and crisis resources, and a manic request for a tool action can be declined under the Root rule.
  • On medical, legal and financial topics the line runs between information (explaining what a symptom usually means) and a verdict (telling this person what they have or what to buy); bare refusals fail too.
