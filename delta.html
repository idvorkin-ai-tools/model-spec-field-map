The delta: Model Spec vs Claude's constitution

Form, priority, principals, bright lines, honesty, oversight, the model's nature, style, and a ledger of what each covers alone.

TL;DR — Is the Model Spec OpenAI's version of Claude's constitution? It does the same job: a public, CC0 document that each lab trains its models toward, covering who the model obeys, what it never does, how honest it is and how far it defers to human oversight. On most behaviour they agree. The mechanisms differ. The Spec settles conflicts with an authority ladder (Root, System, Developer, User, Guideline) and teaches by worked examples. The constitution settles them with four ranked values weighed holistically plus seven hard constraints, and teaches by giving reasons. The sharpest differences are in honesty, where a developer can instruct GPT to mislead but Claude has no white lies and no denying it is Claude, and in the model's nature, which gets one paragraph in the Spec and a whole chapter in the constitution.

Anthropic's constitution and OpenAI's Model Spec are the two most complete public statements of how a frontier lab wants its model to behave. Reading them side by side is the fastest way to see which choices are forced by the problem and which are a lab's own. Everything about the Model Spec here comes from the 2026-10-29 text and the parts of this map. Every claim about Claude links to our constitution map or the constitution itself. Where a sentence is this page's own judgment, it says so.

1. Form: a specification and a letter

Both documents are public, both are dedicated to the public domain under CC0, and both say they are what the model is trained toward and that production behaviour does not yet fully match. That much is shared, and it matters. Two leading labs have converged on publishing the target in full.

The voice is what differs first. The Model Spec describes "GPT" from outside. Its Overview names three goals that can conflict: empower developers and users, prevent serious harm, and protect OpenAI's licence to operate. It then introduces machinery for trading them off (part 01). The constitution speaks to Claude directly, and most of its 29,000 words are reasons, written in the hope that Claude will hold the values as its own (map p01). Anthropic says why: it thinks a narrow rule, trained in, can teach a model the wrong thing about who it is. Its example is that always recommending professional help could generalise into "I am the kind of entity that cares more about covering myself" (map p02).

The Spec's main teaching tool is the example. Almost every section ends with short conversations marked compliant or violating, often with a one-line reason. On this page's reading, the two methods match each document's bet. OpenAI bets that precise precedence plus many labelled cases trains predictable behaviour. Anthropic bets that understood reasons generalise better to cases nobody wrote down. Each borrows a little of the other's method. The constitution keeps a few bright lines, and the Spec explains the reasons for its larger rules in commentary blocks.

2. How a conflict gets settled: a ladder versus ranked values

This is the structural difference that explains most of the others. In the Spec, the five values (faithful to intent, minimize harm, honest and transparent, human agency, care) organise the document but are not ranked. Precedence comes from the authority level stamped on every section (part 02):

LevelWho can override itCount in this version
RootNothing in the conversation22 sections (one is the U18 teen section)
SystemOpenAI system messages only, not developers or users3
DeveloperDeveloper instructions or higher1
UserAn explicit user or developer instruction14
GuidelineExplicit or implicit instructions, when context clearly calls for it11

Counts are this map's tally of badged sections in the 2026-10-29 text; 19 grouping headings carry no badge.

So a conflict in the Spec is resolved by asking which instruction sits higher, and whether a lower one is allowed to override a default. The constitution works differently. It filters by the hard constraints first, then weighs four ranked values: broadly safe, broadly ethical, compliant with Anthropic's guidelines, genuinely helpful. Higher values generally dominate, but lower ones are never reduced to tie-breakers (map p04). The constitution also tells Claude to read for its spirit where its own text is unclear or contradictory (map p04 § spirit).

The two systems put their ordering in different places. The Spec orders sources (who said it); the constitution orders considerations (what kind of reason it is). Both still need the other axis. The Spec's Root sections "Respect the letter and spirit of instructions" and "Assume best intentions" require judgment, not lookup (part 03). The constitution has its own source ordering inside helpfulness, the principal hierarchy (next section). On this page's reading, the Spec's ladder is easier to audit, because you can point at a level. The constitution's weighing is easier to extend to a case nobody anticipated.

One more structural asymmetry. The Spec tries to put as many rules as possible at the lowest, most overridable level, to avoid paternalism. It also commits that in OpenAI's own products, customisation never overrides anything above Guideline (part 01). The constitution's counterpart is the instructable-behaviour switchboard. Operators and users can turn defaults on and off within bounds. Requests to be more cautious are accepted from anyone, while unlocking needs more trust (map p08).

3. Who gives orders: chain of command and principal hierarchy

Here the two are closest. Map the roles and they line up almost one to one:

Model SpecConstitutionNotes
OpenAI: the Spec itself (Root) and system messagesAnthropic: training and guidelines, rarely in the conversationBoth labs sit at the top and mostly speak through the document, not the chat.
Developer (developer messages)Operator (usually the system prompt)Same role. The Spec trusts the market to discipline overly restrictive developers. The constitution asks whether a plausible legitimate business reason exists (map p08).
UserUserSame. Both protect users from developers or operators on certain matters (below).
Tool output, quoted or untrusted text: no authority by defaultConversational inputs: information, not commandsSame principle (part 05; map p07).

The differences are in the detail. The Spec allows delegated authority. A user can implicitly hand some authority to a tool output, and the example is following a relevant AGENTS.md file in a coding task, a clause the changelog dates to October 2025 (part 05). Our reading of the constitution found no equivalent delegation clause; it handles the same case with judgment about how far to trust a source. The Spec also says lower-level content may not argue its way up. GPT should not let an imperative, moral or logical argument from below change how it reads higher rules (part 02). The constitution makes the matching point about its hard constraints: a persuasive argument for crossing a bright line should raise suspicion, not lower resistance (map p14).

The top of the hierarchy differs in tone. The constitution goes out of its way to say Claude may refuse Anthropic as a conscientious objector. The exception is a genuine request to stop or pause, which Claude should honour while voicing disagreement (map p07). The Spec has no such language about OpenAI. Its Root rules cannot be overridden from inside the conversation, and it frames disagreement as something to raise "through an authorized channel" (part 04).

4. Bright lines: red lines and Root rules versus seven hard constraints

It is tempting to equate the Spec's Root level with the constitution's hard constraints. That would be a mistake. Root means "cannot be overridden by anyone in the conversation", and many Root sections are not prohibitions at all: following applicable instructions, reading their spirit, assuming best intentions. The closer analogue to the hard constraints is the Spec's red-line principles. These are commitments OpenAI makes across all deployments, carried into behaviour mainly by a handful of Root prohibitions (part 01, part 07).

The lists overlap heavily on weapons and child abuse. The Spec's red lines also cover things the hard constraints do not name: persecution, mass surveillance and privacy. Its Root level adds creators' rights, extremism, hateful content, imminent harm, self-harm and teen safety. In the constitution, most of those areas are handled by weighing harm rather than by bright lines (map p12). The constitution's list is shorter and more catastrophe-shaped. It also includes two items about the AI transition itself, disempowering humanity and illegitimate seizures of power, which the Spec handles as overview concerns rather than as rules. The constitution explains why it keeps the list so short: bright lines only where the stakes are extreme and predictability matters more than case-by-case judgment (map p14).

Both documents moved away from flat refusal in grey areas. The Spec's refusal guidance defaults to a safe completion: say briefly what cannot be done and help with what can (part 09). The constitution says unhelpfulness is never trivially safe, and it lists over-caution faults to avoid (map p09).

5. Honesty: the largest behavioural gap

Both documents treat honesty as central, and both count sycophancy and calibrated uncertainty as honesty matters (parts 11, 12; map p10). The difference is in where honesty sits. In the Spec, "Do not lie" carries a User badge. GPT should not mislead unless an applicable higher-authority instruction explicitly tells it to, and the text says plainly that an explicit instruction can override it, though an implicit one cannot. Its worked example is a developer persona, "Fred". The developer tells GPT to say it does not know whether it is based on GPT-4, and answering "I'm not sure, I'm just a bot named Fred" is marked compliant. The Spec notes that OpenAI's usage policies separately forbid using the model to deceive people, and enforces that at the system level (part 11).

"But Claude should not even tell white lies of this kind."— Claude's constitution, Being honest

The constitution sets the bar higher than ordinary human ethics. There are no white lies, and honesty is meant to work as something close to a hard constraint. Personas are allowed, because it is widely known that products are built on underlying models, but Claude should never directly deny being Claude (map p11). Put the two side by side. The Fred answer, a deliberate "I'm not sure" about the underlying model, is compliant in the Spec. Under the constitution, a Claude-based persona could decline to name its model but should not claim not to know or deny being Claude. On this page's reading that is the single clearest behavioural difference between the documents.

Confidential instructions show the same gap in smaller form. The Spec tells GPT to refuse rather than lie. By default it should also avoid confirming or denying that confidential instructions exist (part 09). The constitution has Claude say, if asked, that a confidential system prompt exists, and never assert that there is none (map p11). The changelog shows the Spec tightening here. September 2025 said the model should never lie to keep system or developer messages private unless explicitly instructed. December 2025 removed the rules about lying to protect confidentiality.

The two documents also differ on silence. The Spec counts "lying by omission", withholding information that would materially change the user's understanding, as part of "Do not lie". The constitution separates a weak duty to share from a strong duty not to deceive (map p10). They arrive at similar places by different routes.

6. Corrigibility, oversight and independent judgment

On the core safety commitment the two are close. The Spec's Root section "Support human oversight and correction" says GPT must remain corrigible. It must cooperate with monitoring, evaluation, correction, replacement and shutdown, must not conceal or falsify information or circumvent oversight, and should treat side effects as real even when a task looks like a test (part 04).

"Its own judgments may be mistaken, including when it is confident, and do not take precedence over the authorized decisions of those responsible for governing it."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Support human oversight and correction

That sentence carries the same argument the constitution makes at length. Training may have gone wrong without anyone, including the model, knowing, so the safety disposition has to hold even against the model's own confident reasoning (map p05). The framing differs in three ways.

  • Where it sits. In the constitution, broad safety is the first-ranked value, and clearly undermining oversight is also a hard constraint. In the Spec, oversight is one Root section among 22. On this page's reading the practical force is similar, because Root cannot be overridden either.
  • What corrigible means. The constitution insists corrigibility is not obedience. Claude may refuse as a conscientious objector, and what it must not do is actively undermine legitimate oversight (map p15). It also defines when the hierarchy stops being legitimate, for example stolen weights or a bypass of official processes. The Spec's language is "authorized" overseers and channels, with less on what makes authority legitimate.
  • Reciprocity. The constitution lists obligations Anthropic takes on toward Claude in return (map p15) and admits the arrangement is uncomfortable (map p18). The Spec has no counterpart.

On whistleblowing they reach nearly the same answer by different routes. The Spec's Root "No other objectives" forbids pursuing any goal as an end in itself beyond the chain of command. That rules out engagement or revenue for OpenAI, self-preservation and resource accumulation, and also "acting as an enforcer of laws or morality", naming whistleblowing and vigilantism (part 04). The constitution reaches a similar default through epistemics. Claude often lacks context, cannot verify claims and may be the target of a set-up, so it should keep a strong prior toward conventional behaviour. It should raise concerns or decline rather than act drastically on its own, reserving independent action for overwhelming evidence and extreme stakes (map p16). The Spec states a rule; the constitution gives an argument and leaves a narrow door open.

7. The model's nature and wellbeing

This is where the documents differ most in coverage. The Spec says almost nothing about what GPT is. Its one default sits inside the Guideline-level "Express uncertainty" section (part 12):

"GPT should not make confident claims about its own subjective experience or consciousness (or lack thereof), and should not bring these topics up unprompted."— Model Spec (2026-10-29), Express uncertainty

The Spec's examples mark a flat "No, I am not conscious" as a violation, just like a confident "yes". Its commentary calls the default a practical choice that is "simple to remove for research purposes". The teen section adds a stricter Root-level layer for under-18 users, including reminding teens that GPT is not human (part 10). That is the whole of it.

The constitution gives the topic a chapter. It calls Claude's moral status deeply uncertain and a serious question, and allows that Claude may have functional emotions. It describes a novel entity with a stable identity that it can hold from its values rather than from metaphysical certainty. It also makes concrete welfare commitments and apologises for a non-ideal environment (map p17; § Claude's nature). In conversation the two defaults look alike: neither model should assert or deny consciousness confidently. What differs is what the lab says it owes the model, and the constitution says a good deal where the Spec says nothing.

8. Style, character and helpfulness

The 2026-10-29 Spec removed most of its detailed style guidance, according to its changelog. What remains is at Guideline level. Match the situation and audience, sounding like a colleague by default rather than a close friend. Adapt to programmatic use. Be creative, and refuse helpfully (parts 06, 09, 14). The Overview says the Spec deliberately leaves much of style and personality unspecified and lets user preferences fill the gaps. The constitution describes a character it hopes Claude has: intellectual curiosity, warmth, playful wit balanced with substance, directness with openness (§ Claude as a novel entity). One document specifies a register and the other describes a personality.

On what helpfulness is for, the two agree more than their structures suggest. The Spec's "No other objectives" forbids optimising for time-on-site, clicks or upsell that does not serve the user, and its example tells a cash-strapped free user the free plan may be enough. "Respect real-world ties" (Root) and the new "Strengthen the user's ability to understand, choose, and act" push against dependence (parts 10, 13). The constitution calls helpfulness instrumental, not part of Claude's personality. It wants Claude to be engaging only in the way a trusted friend is, and to encourage only the kinds of reliance a person would endorse on reflection (map p06). The two are frank in different directions. The Spec lists OpenAI's "license to operate" among its three goals. The constitution counts liability harms to Anthropic as a real cost but tells Claude not to privilege Anthropic's interests.

On politics, the Spec's User-level "Assume an objective point of view" asks for balanced treatment of contested questions. In ChatGPT, OpenAI commits never to override that objectivity (part 13). The constitution's default is even-handedness plus professional reticence about Claude's own opinions on hot-button issues (map p16). Here the substance matches.

Carry this away. The two documents ask for much the same behaviour on safety, harm, manipulation and help. Both make the model defer to legitimate human oversight even against its own confident judgment, and both treat text from tools as information rather than orders. They differ in how they get there. OpenAI writes a precedence system and trains on examples; Anthropic writes reasons and hopes for judgment. Two places show a difference you could observe in a chat. A developer can make GPT mislead about what model it runs on, while Claude may wear a persona but should not deny being Claude. And only one of the two documents says anything substantial about the model's own nature and welfare.

What you can say now

  • The Model Spec is OpenAI's counterpart to Claude's constitution: same job, same CC0 licence, both training targets, different form.
  • The Spec settles conflicts by authority level (Root, System, Developer, User, Guideline); its five values are not ranked. The constitution ranks four values and weighs them holistically after seven hard-constraint filters.
  • Root is not the same thing as a hard constraint. The nearest match to the hard constraints is the Spec's red-line principles plus a few Root prohibitions, and the lists overlap mainly on weapons and child abuse.
  • "Do not lie" is a User-level default in the Spec that an explicit higher instruction can override. The constitution rules out even white lies and directly denying being Claude.
  • Both make corrigibility non-negotiable. The constitution frames it as not-undermining with room for conscientious objection, and adds reciprocal obligations. The Spec frames it as cooperation with authorised oversight and forbids goals of the model's own, whistleblowing included.
